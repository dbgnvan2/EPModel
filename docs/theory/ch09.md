# Ch09 — The Use of Family Theory in Clinical Practice

## 0. Provenance
- **Stated date / venue (from the text itself):** none stated. No presentation venue, journal, or date appears in the chapter. Internal dating evidence only: he describes the family movement as having begun "in the early and mid 1950s" and family psychiatry as having evolved "[i]n little more than one decade"; he cites his own earlier papers as Bowen 1960, 1961, 1965a; the latest work cited by others is Scheflen (1964); he reports "twelve years" of family experience and that "[f]or the past seven years my practice has been devoted entirely to 'family' psychotherapy."
- **Clinical setting and data source:**
  - Twelve years of family work, "over 10,000 hours of observing families in family psychotherapy."
  - The research "started with schizophrenia" (one member in total dysfunction, patterns too intense to miss), then required "work with the entire range of human dysfunction."
  - The volume of experience is **private practice**: "an average clinical load of forty families are seen with a maximum of thirty hours per week." Few families seen more than once a week; an increasing number do as well with less frequent appointments.
  - Multigenerational postulations were derived from "historical material covering three to four generations on approximately 100 families, and ten or more generations on eight families."
  - For the first five years of family practice he also did individual psychotherapy and had a few patients in psychoanalysis.
- **Genre:** both, weighted to theory. Roughly **60% theory / 40% technique**. Structure: an opening history and status report on "the family movement" (~1/8 of the chapter), then "Theoretical and Clinical Orientation of the Author" and "The Family Theory" (the bulk), then "Clinical Use of Family Psychotherapy" in three named parts (survey of the family fields; the process of differentiation of a self; principles and technique). He states outright that the theory is the point: "the greatest contribution of 'family' will come from the theoretical."

# STREAM A — How families function

## A1. Phenomena requiring explanation

- **Individual theory has no model for a relationship system.** Every early family investigator, on shifting focus from individual to family, hit the same wall and had to invent his own vocabulary for "the distortion and rigidity, the reciprocal functioning, and the 'interlocking,' 'binding,' 'stuck togetherness' of the system." → early family-research investigators, 1950s.
- **Multiple independent investigators converged on similar phenomena with different names.** Lidz "schism and skew" (1957); Wynne "pseudomutuality" (1958); Ackerman "interlocking pathology" (1956); Bateson/Jackson "double bind" (1956); Bowen "undifferentiated family ego mass." "As the years pass, the original concepts tend to be less 'different.'" → the family research field.
- **Seeing families together exposes data that composites of individual interviews obscure.** Family members are simultaneously "being, and doing, and acting, and interacting, and transacting, and communicating, and pretending, and posturing" — so much that "structure and order are hard to see." → daily family observation.
- **Communication runs on many channels at once**, including verbal content, tone/inflection learned in infancy and used "without 'knowing' he knows it," kinesics/body language (Birdwhistell 1952, Scheflen 1964), gross action (Dysinger's 1957 "action dialogue" study blanking out words between a mother and daughter), nonverbal and "extrasensory perception which operates with fair accuracy in some families," and "a visceral response in one family member to anxiety or a mood shift in another." → families in therapy.
- **Families diagnose, classify, and assign characteristics to their own members**, and the assignment then behaves as a fact. Present "to some degree in all families." Assignments that *overvalue* are as unrealistic as those that devalue; the devaluing ones are simply the ones psychiatrists see. → all families observed.
- **Family labels are unreliable as descriptions of the labelled person but reliable as data about the relationship system.** Example: a grandfather called "alcoholic" by two generations of descendants — he was successful and fairly responsible except toward a very anxious wife, stayed away from her, and drank moderately; the wife's label was accepted by the children and passed to the grandchildren. Second example: a man who insisted he had a real drinking problem had lost exactly **one** day of work to drinking. → private practice consultations.
- **Family debates about "rejection," "love," and "hostility" occur with no objective change in the underlying state.** People "react strongly to statements about" love while there is "no objective evidence of change in 'love' within the family." → families in psychotherapy.
- **Dysfunction in one part of a family is accompanied by an equal degree of overfunction in another.** "It is factual that dysfunctioning and overfunctioning exist together." → whole range of family dysfunction.
- **Marked discrepancies in functioning between spouses that puzzle observers** — "people wonder how one spouse so strong would marry another so weak." → clinical observation.
- **A regressed schizophrenic person pulls up to resourceful functioning when his parents are sick, and falls back when they recover.** → the schizophrenia research families.
- **Symptom onsets cluster in time with events elsewhere in the family network.** A boy first played hooky the day his maternal grandmother was hospitalized for feared cancer. A daughter developed nephritis ten days after her mother returned from the maternal grandmother's funeral. A teenaged son went into serious behaviour dysfunction "within weeks" of the sudden death of his father's boss (the father's principal outside emotional tie).
- **Clustered dysfunction in an extended family after one nodal event.** A 65-year-old woman had a radical mastectomy for breast cancer; two weeks later one daughter had a serious car accident requiring months of hospitalization; a grandson's first delinquent act occurred two weeks after the operation and he was arrested six weeks after it; and the daughter-in-law was referred, two months after the operation, for a depression for which hospitalization had been suggested.
- **Families systematically misremember and decouple these coincidences.** "The family emotional system operates always to obscure and misremember and to treat such events as coincidental." Dates come back as "he was about . . . eleven or twelve" and "it was about five or six years ago." In one case the wife later insisted the daughter's illness *preceded* the grandmother's death; the husband's memory and Bowen's notes said otherwise.
- **Families do not present until flexibility is lost.** "Families often do not seek help until flexibility of the system is lost and the functioning of one member is severely impaired."
- **A family can get worse when treated together**: "the 'helpless one' becoming more helpless in response to the overfunctioning of the other."
- **Improving communication produces fast, dramatic, but non-durable change.** Family group therapy can be "amazingly effective as a short-term process"; "[e]ven a slight improvement in communication can produce dramatic shifts in the feeling system, and even a period of exhilaration" — but he "ha[s] not been able to use this as a long-term method for resolving underlying problems."
- **A whole family can improve, terminate pleased, and be unchanged at the level that produced the problem.** In the years he saw both parents plus the symptomatic child, "[f]amily communication improved, symptoms disappeared, and the families would terminate, much pleased with the result. There was no basic change in the pattern of the parental relationship."
- **Families in which the problem stays confined to one area exist and are rare.** A few with severe marital conflict and no impairment of either spouse or of children; a few with no marital conflict, no spouse dysfunction, and the entire marital weight on one child (who may show no significant symptoms until after adolescence, then collapse).
- **Siblings do markedly unequally**: "Siblings who do best are usually least involved in the family emotional system. Those who do poorly are usually most involved."
- **Sibling-position personality profiles (Toman 1961) are "remarkably accurate"** — but are altered in the child who is the object of the family projection process. Observed inversion: of two daughters, the *older* (most involved) emerged with the profile of a younger "baby," and the younger (less involved) with the characteristics of an older daughter.
- **Physical illness clusters at low differentiation**: "A high incidence of physical illness often occurs in those with low levels of differentiation of self."
- **Families come in "exploding" and "cohesive" types.** Example family: the father cut off from his family at college (contact reduced to infrequent brief visits, occasional letters, Christmas cards) and five of his six siblings did the same; the mother maintained frequent letters, gifts, reunions, and visits, and all five of her sibship married spouses drawn into her family's orbit.
- **The therapist himself is inside the phenomenon**: "The 'therapist' also fits into this concept of the family as a system."
- **[p2] People at the very bottom of the scale can live symptom-free.** In the 0–25 band, "[s]ome are able to maintain a sufficient system of dependent attachments to function through life without symptoms. This is more possible for those in the upper part of this group." → symptom presence is dissociable from level even at the extreme low end.
- **[p2] The bottom of the 0–25 band is defined institutionally**: "At the extreme lower end are those who cannot exist outside the protective walls of an institution."
- **[p2] Approach–avoidance in the pre-marital spouses.** Of the 30–35 pair: "Both are vulnerable to the closeness of an intense emotional relationship. Both long for closeness but both are 'allergic' to it."
- **[p2] Fact-gathering is itself therapeutic, as a side effect.** "A secondary dividend of a family field survey is the family's beginning intellectual awareness of related events." The survey is described as being "primarily for the therapist," and this is the one benefit to the family named.

## A2. Mechanisms proposed

- **The family as a system.** "The family is a system in that a change in one part of the system is followed by compensatory change in other parts of the system." He deliberately declines to say what *kind* of system, then, for this theoretical-therapeutic system, treats it as a combination of an **emotional system** ("the force that motivates the system") and a **relationship system** ("the ways it is expressed"; communication, interaction and other modalities are subsumed under relationship).
- **Nesting of systems.** "The functioning of any system is dependent on the functioning of the larger systems of which it is a part, and also on its subsystems." Illustrated by solar system/universe and molecule.
- **Function ranges continuously from overfunction to total failure**, and overfunction itself ranges from *compensated* to *decompensated* — his analogy is the tachycardia of an athlete versus the tachycardia preceding heart failure and death.
- **Overadequate–inadequate reciprocity** (his earlier term, Bowen 1960). One member automatically overfunctions to compensate for another's dysfunction. Flexible and benign when the dysfunction is a temporary illness. In chronic fixed states flexibility is lost and the direction of causation reverses: "in the chronic states there is evidence that the dysfunction appears later to compensate for overfunction in the other." The overfunctioner routinely sees his overfunctioning as *necessitated* by the other's poor functioning.
- **Runaway of the reciprocity.** "When the mechanism advances beyond a certain point, anxiety drives the mechanism toward panic and rapid increase in both overfunction and dysfunction." The pressure can "jam the circuits" of the disabled one into paralysed collapse. Symptoms appear "when the dysfunction approaches nonfunctioning."
- **Reversibility from either pole.** "Even at this point, recovery can begin with the slightest decrease of the overfunctioning, or a slight decrease in the dysfunction."
- **The undifferentiated family ego mass** — the central concept. "[A] conglomerate emotional oneness that exists in all levels of intensity," from most intense to almost imperceptible. An emotional process shifts about within the nuclear family ego mass (father, mother, children) "in definite patterns of emotional responsiveness."
  - *How much a given member is involved* depends on his basic level of involvement in the mass.
  - *How many members are involved* depends on the intensity of the process and on the functional state of individual relationships to the central mass at that moment.
  - Under stress the process can pull in the whole nuclear family, "a whole spectrum of more peripheral family members, and even nonrelatives and representatives of social agencies, clinics, schools, and courts." In calm periods it can stay confined to a small segment, e.g. mother and child with the father isolated from the intense twosome.
- **The closeness cycle within intense relationships.** Three phases: (1) calm, comfortable closeness; (2) anxious, uncomfortable overcloseness, "with the incorporation of the 'self' of one by the 'self' of the other"; (3) distant rejection in which the two "literally repel each other." Some families cycle rapidly; others fix in one phase for years or for life (notably the angry-rejection phase). In the rejection phase, each can retreat into a similar emotional involvement with another family member or with someone outside the family.
- **The triangle as the basic building block.** "The basic building block of any emotional system is the triangle."
  - *Calm state:* two members hold a comfortable alliance; the third is the unfavoured "outsider" and moves either toward winning the favour of one of the others, or toward rejection "which may be planned as winning favor."
  - *Tension state:* the position values invert — the outsider is now the **favoured** position, and "both of the emotionally overinvolved ones will predictably make efforts to involve the third in the conflict."
  - *Triangling:* "When emotional tension in a two-person system exceeds a certain level, it 'triangles' a third person, permitting the tension to shift about within the triangle."
  - *Interlocking:* any two in an existing triangle can add a new triangle; an emotional system is a series of interlocking triangles; "[t]he emotional tension system can shift to any of the old preestablished circuits." Rising tension recruits successively more outside members.
- **The detached-third rule.** "It is a clinical fact that the original two-person tension system will resolve itself automatically when contained within a three-person system, one of whom remains emotionally detached." This is the load-bearing mechanism of the whole method.
- **Emotional chain reaction.** In the least involved situations the process is a subtle chain of emotional responsiveness: "a smile in one family member might initiate an action response in another, and this initiate a reverie about a dream in another, which is followed by a 'change the subject' joke in another."
- **Differentiation of self / undifferentiation ("ego fusion")** as a single continuum for all human functioning (see A4 for the scale).
- **Basic vs functional level of self.** A person has a *basic* level and a *functional* level. "The more intense the degree of ego fusion, the more the 'borrowing' and 'lending' and 'giving' and 'sharing' of self within the family ego mass. The more the shifting of 'strength' within the ego mass, the more likely the marked discrepancies in functional levels of self." Shifts can be brief (the schizophrenic person rising when his parents fall ill) or fixed for life (see the 55/15 marriage in A4). Functional shifts are large in the lower half of the scale and "almost no functional shifts" occur high on the scale.
- **[p2] Two named pseudo-autonomy strategies, adopted after adolescence and carried into the marriage.** Of the 30–35 spouses: "As children, both spouses were dependently attached to parents. After adolescence, in an effort to function autonomously, they *either denied the dependence while still living at home, or they used separation and physical distance to achieve autonomy*." Both then "function relatively well as long as they keep relationships distant or casual." This is a two-type taxonomy of how attachment to the family of origin is (not) resolved, stated before the marriage forms, and it is the earliest form in the corpus of what later becomes the cutoff taxonomy. Pass 1 recorded only the downstream consequence ("the one who ran away from his own family will tend to run away in the marriage") and missed the taxonomy itself.
- **[p2] The therapist as "expert" and "engineer."** "[T]he therapist became a kind of 'expert' in understanding family systems and an 'engineer' in helping the family restore itself to functioning equilibrium." An explicitly mechanical role description, sitting alongside the consultant/supervisor terms pass 1 recorded and in tension with the anti-repairman rule in B2.
- **Marriage fuses two pseudo-selfs.** Spouses "fuse together into a 'new family ego mass' with obliteration of ego boundaries and incorporation of the two 'pseudo-selfs' into a 'common self.'" The marriage "duplicates essential characteristics of former ego masses," and each spouse deploys against the other the mechanisms he used in his family of origin ("the one who ran away from his own family will tend to run away in the marriage"). The most common mechanism is enough emotional distance for each to function on a reasonable level of pseudo-self.
- **Three mechanisms *within* the nuclear family ego mass for controlling the intensity of the ego fusion** (enumerated exactly as three):
  1. **Marital conflict** — "each spouse fights for an equal share of the common self and neither gives in to the other."
  2. **Dysfunction in one spouse** — usually a brief conflict then one "gives in" to relieve it (both usually see *self* as the one giving in, but one does more of it); or one "volunteers to be the 'no-self'" in support of the other, who becomes dependent on him. The one who loses self may sink to a level at which he becomes "candidates for physical, emotional, or social illness." Some marriages run for years with one functioning well and the other chronically ill.
  3. **Transmission of the problem to one or more children** — "one of the most common mechanisms."
- **A conserved quantity of immaturity.** "The system operates as if there is a certain amount of 'immaturity' to be absorbed by the system." Large quantities may be "bound" by serious dysfunction in one member; "[o]ne chronically ill parent can be a kind of 'protection' against serious impairment of children." In most families the problem is spread across all three areas. Where the quantity is very large there can be maximum marital conflict *and* severe spouse dysfunction *and* maximum child involvement *and* conflict with families of origin, and "still free-floating 'immaturity.'"
- **The family projection process.** A family problem is transmitted to one member "by years of nagging pronouncements, and then fixed there with a diagnosis." Within the family it "focuses on certain children and leaves others relatively uninvolved." It usually runs between mother and child, and its onset is often marked in the mother's fantasy system: in a significant projection process, "her worries and concerns have fixed on the child since the pregnancy, her relationship with this one has been 'different,' she has long worried about it, and she is eager to talk about it."
- **The projection process is energetically conserved and redistributes load.** "This much psychic energy from mother to child will change the psychic energy system in the family. It might serve to reduce marital conflict, but it might also disturb the husband to the point he would start spending longer hours at work, or he might begin drinking, or have an affair, or become emotionally closer to his parents."
- **Assignment becomes fact.** The diagnosed one "may resist the family pronouncement and precipitate a family debate; or he may alternately resist and accept; or he may invite it, at which time the assigned characteristic becomes an operational fact."
- **Mechanisms *outside* the nuclear ego mass — extended family coupling.** With significant ego fusion there is "a borrowing and sharing of ego strength between the nuclear family and the family of origin." Under stress the nuclear family can be *stabilized* by contact with a family of origin, and can equally be *disturbed* by stress in it. "In general, the intensity of the process in a nuclear family is attenuated by active contacts with the families of origin."
- **Exploding vs cohesive families.** Some families disperse (members separate physically from the family of origin); others hold members and pull in the spouses their children marry.
- **Cutoff does not resolve attachment.** "The spouse who separates from his family of origin does not resolve the emotional attachment. The old relationship remains 'latent' and can be revived with emotional contact."
- **Substitution of outside systems when both spouses are cut off.** When both spouses have detached from their families of origin, they are "usually much more dependent on each other, and the emotional process in the family tends to be more intense"; such families "tend to become more invested in the emotional systems of work and social situations" (e.g. a father's long-term emotional dependence on his boss).
- **Multigenerational transmission process.** Parents transmit varying levels of their immaturity to their children; the child most involved emerges below the parents' basic level, the child who grows up "relatively outside the emotional demands and pressures of the family ego mass" emerges above it, and a middle child can emerge at the parents' own level. Iterated over generations, one line descends toward "no-self" and another ascends.
- **Sibling position determines personality profile** (Toman 1961, incorporated wholesale), *modified* by the family projection process, which can invert the expected profile.
- **Emotional system / feeling system / intellectual system as a three-layer stack.** "Operationally I regard an emotional system as something deep that is in contact with cellular and somatic processes, and a feeling system as a bridge that is in contact with parts of the emotional system on one side and with the intellectual system on the other." He also draws a working distinction between **feelings** (subjective awareness) and **opinions** (logic and reasoning of the intellectual system), noting people routinely say "I feel that . . ." when they mean "I believe that . . . ."
- **Emotional functioning is biological and law-governed.** Man is "an evolutionary assemblage of cells"; emotional illness "a disorder of man's emotional system," which is "basically related to man's protoplasmic being." "There are emotional mechanisms as automatic as a reflex and that occur as predictably as the force that causes the sunflower to keep its face toward the sun." The obstacle to understanding is not complexity but denial: the difficulty "is governed more by man's reasoning that denies its existence than by the complexity of the system."
- **Basic premise about self-report:** "what man thinks about himself, and what he says about himself, is different in many important ways from what he is."
- **Rejection as an equilibrium mechanism** — not a pathology. "As I see 'rejection,' it is one of the most useful mechanisms for maintaining equilibrium in a relationship system. It goes on constantly between people, usually unmentioned."
- **The togetherness force actively resists differentiation.** "An emotional system operates with a delicately balanced equilibrium in which each devotes a certain amount of being and self to the welfare and well-being of the others. In a state of disequilibrium, the family system operates automatically to restore the former togetherness equilibrium, though this be at the expense of some." When a member differentiates, "the togetherness forces oppose with vigor," and the opposition appears in *all* interlocking systems — the nuclear family, both extended families, and work and social systems.
- **Allies are a trap.** Seeking an ally or group against the system produces "a new undifferentiated oneness with his allies (even a sect or minority group within the larger system) from which it is harder to differentiate than from the original oneness."
- **The "fraudulent" emotional contract of the conflictual marriage.** It begins with "an almost idyllic model in which each devoted a high percentage of 'self' to the happiness and well-being of the other" — an agreement neither can keep. Because functioning of self is then dependent on the other, "any failure in happiness or functioning is the fault of the other." The investment does not decrease; "only it shifts into negative energy that accuses, indicts, and diagnoses." Hence conflictual marriages are *enduring*, and the amount of thinking time invested in the other is probably greater than in calm marriages.
- **Adaptiveness runs all the way down to the cell.** The more adaptive spouse (usually the more passive) is not merely surface-compliant: "[t]his involves the entire fantasy, feeling, and action system. A spouse who develops physical symptoms in response to an emotional field is in a 'cell to cell' adaptiveness that is deep."
- **Symptom equivalence.** "Symptoms in any part of the family are viewed as evidence of dysfunction, whether the symptoms be emotional, physical, conflictual, or social." The broad family patterns "are also present in physical illness and social dysfunction such as irresponsible behavior and delinquency."
- **Detriangling the triangle.** One parent establishes an "I" position and differentiates a self in the relationship with the child; the other parent and the child then "fuse together into a more intense oneness that alternately attacks and pleads with the 'differentiating parent' to rejoin the oneness"; if the differentiating one holds a reasonable "I" for even a few days, there is "an automatic decrease in the intensity of the attachment between the other two and a permanent decrease in the intensity of the triangle." Then the other parent does the same, then another cycle with each parent separately.

## A3. Predictive claims

Bowen's mode throughout is assertion from clinical experience; he offers case illustrations rather than controlled evidence, and explicitly refuses causal claims about coincident events (see X2). Flagged below as **[asserted]**, **[case-illustrated]**, or **[explicitly refused as causal]**.

**Reciprocity and symptom formation**
1. If dysfunction is present in one part of a family, then an equal degree of overfunction is present elsewhere in it. **[asserted as fact]**
2. If overfunction–dysfunction becomes chronic and fixed rather than temporary, then the dysfunction appears *later*, to compensate for the other's overfunction (i.e. the causal arrow reverses relative to the acute case). **[asserted, "there is evidence"]**
3. If dysfunction approaches nonfunctioning, then symptoms develop. **[asserted]**
4. If the reciprocating mechanism advances beyond a certain point, then anxiety drives it toward panic and both overfunction and dysfunction increase rapidly; increased pressure can jam the disabled one into paralysed collapse. **[asserted]**
5. If either the overfunctioning *or* the dysfunctioning decreases even slightly, then recovery can begin — even from collapse. **[asserted]**
6. If the overfunctioning one and the poorly functioning one are compared, then it is "far easier for the overfunctioning one to 'tone down' the overfunctioning than for the poorly functioning one to 'pull up.'" **[asserted from experience]**

**Triangles**
7. If emotional tension in a two-person system exceeds a certain level, then a third person is triangled in. **[asserted]**
8. If tension increases further, then increasing numbers of outside members are involved via interlocking triangles, and the tension can shift onto any preestablished circuit. **[asserted]**
9. If a two-person tension system is contained within a three-person system in which one remains emotionally detached, then the original tension "will resolve itself automatically." **[asserted as "a clinical fact"]**
10. In calm periods, the third position is the unfavoured one; in tension, the third position is the *favoured* one and the two overinvolved ones will "predictably" try to draw the third in. **[asserted]**
11. If one parent establishes and holds an "I" position in the triangle with the child for even a few days, then there is an automatic decrease in the intensity of the attachment between the other parent and the child, and a *permanent* decrease in the intensity of that triangle. **[asserted from experience]**

**Differentiation level as predictor**
12. The lower a person is on the scale, then: the more he holds to religious dogma, cultural values, superstition and outmoded beliefs and the less able he is to discard rigidly held ideas; the more he makes "a 'federal case' of rejection, lack of love, and injustice" and demands recompense; the more he holds the other responsible for his self and happiness; the more intense the ego fusions and the more extreme the mechanisms used to control "too much closeness" (emotional distance, isolation, conflict, violence, physical illness); the higher the incidence of being in touch with the intrapsychic of the other and intuitively knowing what the other thinks and feels; and the more the impairment in meaningful communication. **[asserted]**
13. The lower the general level of differentiation in a family, the greater the frequency and intensity of time-related events across the family network. **[asserted]**
14. If a person is in the 0–25 band, then he is easily stressed into emotional disequilibrium and dysfunction can be long or permanent; and in family psychotherapy Bowen has "yet to see a person in this group attain a higher 'basic' level of differentiation of self." **[asserted from 12 years' practice]**
15. If a person is in the lower part of the 25–50 band and under stress, then transient psychotic episodes, delinquency problems and symptoms of that intensity develop; in the upper range, neurotic problems. **[asserted, with the caveat that correlating the scale with clinical categories is "misleading"]**
16. If a person is in the 50–75 band and under sufficient stress, then he can compromise principle and make feeling decisions, and can develop fairly severe emotional or physical symptoms — but symptoms are more episodic and recovery much faster. **[asserted]**
17. If differentiation is high (85–95), then functioning is unaffected by praise or criticism; the person marries a spouse of equal differentiation; and both can relax ego boundaries for intense shared experience and disengage at will. **[asserted — but note he says he has *never seen* anyone in the 75–100 band clinically]**
18. The more intense the ego fusion, the greater the borrowing/lending of self and hence the greater the discrepancies between functional levels within the family. **[asserted]**
19. Low differentiation predicts a high incidence of *physical* illness. **[asserted]**
20. Sibling-position personality profiles (Toman) are most accurate for people in the **mid-scale** range; and the higher a person on the scale, the more adult (rather than infantile) qualities predominate in his profile. **[asserted]**

**Nuclear family mechanisms**
21. If a large quantity of immaturity is bound by serious chronic dysfunction in one parent, then that operates as "a kind of 'protection' against serious impairment of children." **[asserted]**
22. Marital conflict does not by itself cause problems in children — evidenced by families with intense marital conflict and no impairment of children. **[case-based negative claim]**
23. Impairment of children can occur without conflict — evidenced by serious impairment developing in calm, harmonious marriages. **[case-based]**
24. If a family projects the entire marital problem into one child with no marital conflict and no spouse dysfunction, then there may be no significant symptoms until after adolescence, when the child collapses into psychotic or comparable dysfunction. **[asserted]**
25. If a projection process is intense and long-term (fixed since pregnancy), then the problem in the child is deeper and more serious; if it started later (e.g. after the death of an important family member), then it is much less serious and much easier to handle in family psychotherapy. **[asserted]**
26. If the mother's psychic energy shifts to a child, then marital conflict may reduce *and* the husband may respond by working longer hours, drinking, having an affair, or moving closer to his parents. **[asserted]**

**Extended family / outside systems**
27. If a nuclear family maintains active contact with the families of origin, then the intensity of the process within the nuclear family is attenuated. **[asserted]**
28. If both spouses are emotionally separated from their families of origin, then they are much more dependent on each other and the family's emotional process is more intense; such families invest more in work and social emotional systems. **[asserted]**
29. If the principal outside emotional tie is lost (e.g. death of a father's boss), then dysfunction appears inside the nuclear family within weeks. **[case-illustrated: teenaged son with behaviour problem]**
30. If symptoms develop slowly, then they are likely the product of slow buildup in the nuclear family; if quickly, then the extended family should be explored, and if it *is* a response to the extended family, it can be regarded as "acute" and "it is fairly easy to restore the family functioning." **[asserted, case-illustrated by the mastectomy cluster]**
31. Cut-off does not extinguish the attachment; contact will revive the latent relationship. **[asserted]**

**Multigenerational**
32. If projection concentrates on one child per generation at maximum intensity, then the schematic runs 50 → 35 → 20 → 10 and 15, and "it requires at least three generations for a person to acquire the level of 'no-self' for a later collapse into schizophrenia." **[asserted, schematic illustration only]** **[p2 correction]** Pass 1 stated a rate of "about 15 points per generation." **That figure is nowhere in the chapter and it is wrong as arithmetic**: the stated steps are −15, −15, then −10 and −5, and the third generation's parents are only described as the 20 marrying "a spouse whose 'needs' for another are as great as his," with no level assigned — so no per-generation rate is determinable from the text. Bowen also caps the schematic explicitly: "**[i]n the average situation the immaturity would progress at a much slower rate**," and the upward line likewise, "in the average family the upward progression is much slower than illustrated in this example." The schematic is an upper bound on rate, not a rate.
33. If a child grows up relatively outside the family ego mass, then he emerges at a level *higher* than his parents'. "[I]n every generation there are children who progress up the scale." **[asserted]**
33b. **[p2]** If a person is in the 0–25 band but can assemble and hold "a sufficient system of dependent attachments," then he can "function through life without symptoms" — more likely in the upper part of that band. **[asserted]** *This is a low-level, symptom-free state, and the model has no way to produce one.*
34. Below about 20, "the problems are too great to be contained in one area" — such a couple will probably have marital, health, and social problems, and the projection will spread to more than one child. **[asserted]**

**Change and its opposition**
35. If an individual moves toward higher differentiation, then the equilibrium is disturbed and the togetherness forces oppose vigorously — in the family, in both extended families, and in work and social systems. **[asserted]**
36. If the individual maintains the stand in spite of opposition, then "the family later applauds." **[asserted]**
37. If someone seeks an ally to help oppose the system, then he ends fused with the ally, in a oneness harder to differentiate from than the original. **[asserted]**
38. If differentiation gets started in one family member, then it can loosen up the entire family system. **[asserted — the core wager of the one-person method]**
39. The highest level of differentiation possible for a family equals the highest level any one member can attain *and maintain* against the emotional opposition of the family unit he lives in. **[asserted as a general principle]**
40. If parents change the parental relationship, then the entire family system changes. **[stated as the "premise" on which he removed children from the sessions; supported by the outcome that children became symptom-free in weeks to months]**

- Generation 3: the 20 marries → children at **10**, **15**, and one outside the mass at **30**. "The ones at 10 and 15 are good candidates for total functional collapse into states such as schizophrenia or criminal behavior."

- Data base: "historical material covering three to four generations on approximately 100 families, and ten or more generations on eight families."

- Survey of the family fields: initially "a number of hours"; with practice, careful structuring and "an average uncomplicated family," "it is possible to do a survey adequately for planning the psychotherapy in one hour."

- Old method (both parents + symptomatic child): "[a]n average course would continue a year or more."

## A5. Terminology

*Terms as used in **this** chapter.*

- **Family movement** — the emergence of family study/therapy since the early-to-mid 1950s, characterized as "a 'movement,'" currently in "a 'healthy, unstructured state of chaos.'"
- **Family therapy** — used so broadly across methods that "the term is meaningless without further description or definition."
- **Family group therapy** — his name for the widely-held popular model: whole families meeting with a therapist who sits alongside to facilitate communication and make observations and interpretations. Effective short-term, not usable long-term for underlying problems.
- **Family psychotherapy** — *his* method, "designed to help individuals differentiate themselves from the 'mass.'" Definition shifted during his career: originally reserved for "the process when two or more family members were seen together," with "individual psychotherapy" for a single person; **now** defined by orientation, not attendance — "a theoretical system that 'thinks' in terms of family and works toward improving the family system is family psychotherapy." He notes the term contains "inaccuracies" but keeps it as "the best working compromise."
- **Marital therapy / individual therapy** — the field's usage: two spouses seen together; one member seen. Reported, not adopted.
- **Undifferentiated family ego mass** — "a conglomerate emotional oneness," the central concept. He grants the term "has been more utilitarian than accurate" — "[p]recisely defined, the four words do not belong together" — but keeps it because it communicates and because each word gives latitude for theoretical extension.
- **Family ego mass** — "[f]or practical purposes... refers to the nuclear family which includes the father, mother, and children of the present and future generations." Also used as **parental ego mass** / **central ego mass**.
- **Nuclear family** — father, mother, and children.
- **Extended family** — "the entire network of living relatives," though clinically usually the three-generation grandparent–parent–child system.
- **Emotional field** — "the emotional process in any area being considered at the moment." Fields "intergear" and are likened to "two constantly changing magnetic fields that influence each other."
- **Emotional system** — the force that motivates the family system; "something deep that is in contact with cellular and somatic processes."
- **Relationship system** — the ways the emotional system is expressed; subsumes communication, interaction and other modalities.
- **Feeling system** — "a bridge that is in contact with parts of the emotional system on one side and with the intellectual system on the other."
- **Intellectual system** — the seat of logic, reasoning, and **opinions**, distinguished operationally from **feelings** (subjective awareness).
- **Differentiation of self** — a person's degree of separateness of intellectual from feeling functioning and of self from the family ego mass; scored 0–100.
- **Undifferentiation / ego fusion** — the opposite pole; used interchangeably here.
- **Differentiation of Self Scale** — the 0–100 continuum for all human functioning. "This theory does not have a concept of 'normal.'"
- **Basic level** vs **functional level** of differentiation — the durable level vs the moment-to-moment level inflated or deflated by borrowing and lending of self.
- **"No-self"** — a person at the bottom of the scale, lacking beliefs and convictions of his own; also used for the spouse who "volunteers to be the 'no-self.'"
- **Pseudo-self** — the self a person operates with in fusion; two pseudo-selfs merge into a "common self" in marriage.
- **Differentiated "I"** — "I am—I believe—I will do—I will not do," contrasted with the narcissistic "I want—I am hurt—I want my rights."
- **Transilient personalities** — "no-selfs" who, lacking their own convictions, "adapt quickly to the prevailing ideology" and borrow outside authority ("Science has shown . . ."). **[p2 precision]** Pass 1 called these "low-scale"; the chapter locates them specifically "[i]n the lower part of this group" — i.e. the lower part of the **25–50** band, not the 0–25 band.
- **Inner directed** — borrowed descriptor applied to the 85–95 group.
- **Triangle** — "[t]he basic building block of any emotional system." He notes it was "not included with the other concepts because it has more to do with therapy than the basic theory."
- **Triangling** — a two-person tension system recruiting a third; also what happens if a therapist sees spouses alternately.
- **Interlocking triangles** — the network structure of an emotional system; "old preestablished circuits."
- **Detriangling the triangle** — his therapeutic operation of helping one parent take an "I" position and differentiate in the triangle with the child.
- **Family projection process** — transmission of a family problem to one member by years of nagging pronouncements, then fixed with a diagnosis (referred to a prior paper, 1965a).
- **Multigenerational transmission process** — the descent/ascent of differentiation level across generations via projection.
- **Overadequate–inadequate reciprocity** — his earlier (Bowen 1960) name for the overfunction–dysfunction pairing.
- **Exploding families / cohesive families** — families whose members disperse from the family of origin vs families that hold members and absorb in-marrying spouses.
- **"Fraudulent" emotional contract** — the impossible mutual pledge of self at the start of the conflictual marriage.
- **Survey of the family fields** — his structured initial-interview fact-gathering process.
- **"Fix"** — a full snapshot at a nodal event: place, date, ages of everyone in the household, functioning of each, and a check on realities in the extended families.
- **Staying out of the transference** — keeping himself emotionally disengaged so the emotional process stays inside the family.
- **Research observers** — the role he assigns family members: to observe and report on self.
- **System experts** — what he wants family members to become, so the family can readjust itself without an outside expert.
- **Consultant / supervisor** — the roles he establishes for himself, deliberately in place of **therapist**, **healer**, **repairman**.
- **Gut reaction** — a parent's "insides tie into knots" in response to discomfort in the child.
- **[p2] "Natural systems"** — appears once, and it is the seed of the later name for the whole theory: "**Man's family is a system which I believe follows the laws of natural systems.**" There is no "natural systems theory" as a named theory here; the phrase is used as a claim about the family, not as a label for his own framework.
- **[p2] "Relationship system in the nuclear family ego mass"** — the second major concept's own running heading, and the operative name of that concept in this chapter. The later name "nuclear family emotional system" does not appear.
- **[p2] "Expert" / "engineer"** — role labels Bowen applies to himself ("an 'engineer' in helping the family restore itself to functioning equilibrium"), distinct from and less prominent than consultant/supervisor, and never retracted here.
- **[p2] "Family dysfunction"** — offered as the more accurate replacement for "emotional illness" but *not adopted*: he writes "the family theory of emotional illness. It would be more accurate to say 'family dysfunction'" and then continues using the former.
- Terms he flags as *misused* and requiring reevaluation: **rejection**, **love**, **hostility**, **masculine**, **feminine**, **aggressive**, **passive**, **homosexual**, **alcoholic**; and the psychiatric jargon he banned within his research staff — **depressed**, **hysterical**, **compulsive**.
- Other investigators' terms recorded but not adopted: **schism and skew** (Lidz), **pseudomutuality** (Wynne), **interlocking pathology** (Ackerman), **double bind** (Bateson/Jackson), **conjoint family therapy** (Jackson), **kinesics** (Birdwhistell/Scheflen).

**Terms doing a job done elsewhere by a different term:** "undifferentiated family ego mass" here carries the work later split among fusion, nuclear family emotional system, and family projection; "emotional illness" is repeatedly glossed as what "would be more accurate to say 'family dysfunction'"; the *within*-family mechanisms are **three** (marital conflict, spouse dysfunction, projection to children) — emotional distance is described as "[t]he most common mechanism" spouses use but is *not* listed as one of the three; and the triangle is explicitly **excluded** from the three major theoretical concepts and assigned to therapy.

Plus **triangles**, held out as a fourth concept belonging to therapy, and Toman's **sibling position** profiles, "combined with my own work" and "used with every family in psychotherapy."

## A6. Structure and roles

- **The nuclear family** is father, mother, and children. The birth of the first child "changes the family from a two-person to a three-person system" — a structural transition he treats as a nodal event worth a full "fix."
- **The symbiotic mother–child pair** is "a fragment of one of the most intense versions" of the ego mass; "[t]he father is equally involved with the mother and child, and other children are involved with varying lesser degrees of intensity." In calm periods the process can contain itself in the mother–child twosome "with the father isolated from the intense twosome."
- **The symptom-bearer** is selected, not intrinsic: the projection process "focuses on certain children and leaves others relatively uninvolved."
- **Sibling position is a primary determinant of personality** (Toman), and it is "one of the most important bits of information" in the extended-family survey. But it is **overwritten by projection**: the older of two daughters, most involved, emerged with a younger-"baby" profile; the less involved younger emerged with older-daughter characteristics. Toman studied "normal" families and "did not consider the personality alterations of the child who was the object of the family projection process."
- **Position within the sibship predicts outcome**: "Siblings who do best are usually least involved in the family emotional system. Those who do poorly are usually most involved."
- **The child raised outside the emotional demands of the ego mass rises above the parents' level**; the maximally involved child falls below it.
- **The overfunctioner and the dysfunctioner** are a paired role set — e.g. "the dominating (overfunctioning) mother and passive father"; the "overadequate husband" and the housebound wife.
- **The adaptive spouse** — "usually the more passive" — is identified in the survey as the one who yields at the level of fantasy, feeling and action, not just surface behaviour, and who may express adaptation somatically.
- **The chronically ill parent** occupies a load-bearing role: binding large quantities of immaturity and thereby "protecting" the children.
- **Who moves first:** any differentiation effort "is for the individual alone." In a conflictual marriage he asks to see "the healthiest, best-integrated one," and if progress stops he shifts to the other spouse. In a sick-spouse marriage, if the well one seeks help, the sick one is near collapse and he works with the well one *about his problems with the sick one*. In detriangling, **one parent** moves first, then the other, then each again in turn. Where a family is worsening under joint work, he works with **one side of the reciprocity** — preferably the overfunctioner, because toning down is easier than pulling up.
- **The therapist's position** is a role in the structure: the potential third point of a triangle who remains emotionally detached, occupying that slot as "consultant" then "supervisor," never as therapist/healer/repairman.
- **Roles the family assigns**: it "diagnoses, classifies, and assigns characteristics to certain family members," and the assignment can become "an operational fact."
- **Extended-family position**: grandparents' occupation, marital relationship and health are "key clues to that family ego mass"; each sibling's birth order, dates, occupation, residence, life course and frequency/nature of contact locate the nuclear parent within his family of origin.
- **Non-family occupants of family positions**: under stress the process pulls in "nonrelatives and representatives of social agencies, clinics, schools, and courts"; a boss can be the principal outside emotional tie.
- **[p2] Material and economic facts are recorded as *survey data and nodal events*, never as a per-person resource.** Occupation appears three times as a fact to be collected — "[t]he occupation of the grandfather," each sibling's "occupation, place of residence" — and economic events appear once, in a list of what perturbs the parental ego mass: "changes in residence, **the purchase of a home**, and occupational success or failure." The only other economic mentions are a socioeconomic *correlate* of the 0–25 band ("the 'dead enders' of society, many of the lower socioeconomic group, and those from higher socioeconomic groups with intense ego fusions") and work success treated as an *output* of the relationship system rather than an input: in the 25–50 band, "[s]uccess in business or professional pursuits is determined more by approval from superiors and from the relationship system than the inherent value of their work." **No person in this chapter has money, income, wealth, or any other material stock.**

# STREAM B — How to do therapy  (→ the coaching agent)

## B1. What the therapist does

1. **Run a "survey of the family fields" in the initial interview with every family**, structured to yield in about one hour enough to formulate the family's overall functioning patterns "for at least two generations" and to plan the psychotherapy.
   - See **both parents** first (more information; plus a working view of the marriage). If marital discord would obstruct fact-gathering, see instead the one parent with the most knowledge of the family.
   - Start with **a chronological review of symptom development** in the identified person, "with specific dates and circumstances at the time of each symptom eruption."
   - Then the **functioning of the parental ego mass since marriage**, chronologically, as it has interacted with outside forces: closeness/distance and contact with extended families, changes of residence, purchase of a home, occupational success or failure, births, serious illness or death in the extended family.
   - Probe the **three stress areas** — marital conflict, illness or other dysfunction, projection to a child — and **get the dates of changes**.
   - At important events (notably the birth of the first child) do a **"fix"**: place, date, ages of everyone in the household, the functioning of each, and the state of the extended families. Get readings on "feeling-fantasy systems of various family members at stress points" where possible.
   - **Check the projection process by asking about the mother's fantasy system before and after the birth of the child.**
   - Then survey **both extended family fields**, in either order: grandfathers' occupations, grandparents' marital relationship and health; per sibling — birth order, exact dates of birth, occupation, residence, a few words about spouse and children, overall life course, and frequency and nature of contact.
   - **Get exact dates by persistent questioning and arithmetic**: "It requires persistent questioning and mathematical computation to get specific information."
   - "It is always helpful to go back as many generations as possible."
2. **Establish the family orientation from the first contact.** Stand alongside the family to help them understand and modify the system.
3. **Define himself to the family**, continuously — starting with an explicit statement of this theoretical and therapeutic system "and its differences from others," and continuing "in almost every session around all kinds of life issues." Take **"action" stands** about "what I will do and will not do."
4. **Teach explicitly**, using clinical examples and "a blackboard for diagrams" in the early sessions of one-person work.
5. **Tell new families about the successes and failures of former families**, to save them "endless hours and months of 'trial and error' experimentation."
6. **Forecast the predictable stages** of a successful differentiation effort so the person can recognise them when they arrive (see B3).
7. **Assign the person the task of becoming a "research observer,"** and structure the hour so that "a major part of each hour will go into their report on their efforts to see self."
8. **Hand principles over as offerings, not prescriptions**: tell them others have found some of these helpful; that the effort will fail if they adopt them without incorporating them into "self" as their own beliefs; that trying something they cannot really believe in is unrealistic; and that it is their responsibility to find other ideas if these do not fit.
9. **Stay emotionally detached** — occupy the third point of the triangle without being absorbed. He calls this "staying out of the transference."
10. **Optimum format: start with husband and wife together and continue with both for the entire course.**
11. **When joint work stalls or worsens the family, work with one side of the reciprocity** — preferentially the *overfunctioning* one, if motivated, "in which the goal is to free the immobilized system and restore enough flexibility for the family to work together."
12. **In cyclical conflictual marriages,** first make "a vigorous effort to help them contain the cycle"; if that fails, state that the pattern is nonproductive, that he is unwilling to spend time this way, and ask to see "the healthiest, best-integrated one" alone.
13. **Ask parents to leave the symptomatic child at home and focus on their own problems.**
14. **Coach the one-person effort concretely**: withdraw psychic energy from the other and invest it in one's own poorly defined ego boundaries; "get off the back" of the other by reducing other-directed thinking, words and action aimed at changing him; listen to the attacks of the other without responding; live with "what is" without trying to change it; define one's own beliefs and convictions without attacking the other's; observe the part self plays in the situation.
15. **In detriangling, help one parent establish an "I" position and differentiate a self in the relationship with the child**, then repeat with the other parent, then cycle again with each separately.
16. **Do a "trial run" with both parents together even in severe triangling**, to test the flexibility of the parental relationship.
17. **With a sick spouse who seeks help**, hold a detached "Let's examine this and understand your part in the family problem," and **propose "family" early** while waiting for the sick one's self to be able to operate in the other's presence.
18. **With a well spouse who seeks help for a collapsing partner**, avoid a relationship with the sick side of the family and relate to the well one "about his problems with the sick one."
19. **If progress stops** because energy has gone into the therapist, **shift the effort to the other spouse.**
20. **Let the calendar do the work**: "My goal is to keep asking questions and let the calendar 'speak' when others are able to 'hear.'"
21. **Record family accounts as reports, not facts**: "I avoid glib dynamic speculations and record the family explanations as, 'The family member said. . . .'"
22. **Treat every family as a research family** — an orientation that came from noticing "research" families did better than "therapy" families.
23. **Train by direct observation**: "In the training of young therapists, considerable experience in observing multiple family members together is essential."

## B2. What the therapist must NOT do

- **Do not diagnose any family member**, and avoid medical-model concepts such as "sick" or "patient."
- **Do not let the family cast him as a "therapist."** "I persistently oppose the tendency of the family to view me as a 'therapist.'"
- **Do not become a "healer" or "repairman"** — "the family goes into dysfunction to wait for the therapist to accomplish his work."
- **Do not enter the transference.** The therapist must not become the object in whom psychic energy is invested; that is the same life style that produced the problem.
- **Do not see spouses alternately.** "It invites 'triangling,' neither really works at the problem, each expects the other to do it, and each tends to justify self to the therapist."
- **Do not run the "differentiation of a self" approach with two spouses at once.** "It is not possible... It results in intense 'triangling.'"
- **Do not frame "I" stands as advice.** "My 'I' stands, all based on experience, are in terms of what I will do and will not do, and are never in terms of 'what is best.'"
- **Do not ask a family to do what he does not do himself.** "I believe a therapist is in poor position to ask a family to do something he does not do."
- **Do not show the family the coincident-events pattern early.** "I have never been able to use the related events early in psychotherapy... Some families found reason to never return."
- **Do not postulate causality** about coincident events, and do not go beyond noting the striking time sequence.
- **Do not bring the other spouse into a sick spouse's therapy too early** — the effort "may terminate within a few hours."
- **Do not treat "the healthiest" family member as a patient.** A therapist who tries to *treat* the healthiest with a medical orientation "could either drive him away or make him into a 'patient.'"
- **Do not tolerate the accuse/excuse cycle indefinitely** — say it is nonproductive and refuse to spend time on it.
- **Do not use psychiatric jargon.** "An effort was made to prohibit the use of psychiatric jargon within the research staff and to use simple descriptive words."
- **Do not treat family labels as facts.** "It can be grossly inaccurate to assign fact to statements such as, 'He was an alcoholic.'"
- **Do not work with segments of families before knowing the whole**: "it is not advisable to work with segments of families until one has a working knowledge of the whole."
- **Do not expect to teach a family member the differentiation principles as borrowed rules** — they will fail if not made the person's own.
- (Implicit prohibition on his own earlier practice) **Do not keep the symptomatic child in the room** if the goal is basic change in the parental relationship.

## B3. Sequencing and preconditions

- **Precondition on the therapist:** he must have dealt with his own emotional functioning and developed technique sufficient to avoid a transference. Bowen dates his own capacity to do "family" psychotherapy with one person to the point at which this became possible; before that there was an "either–or" distinction between family and individual work.
- **Precondition on training:** direct observation of multiple family members together comes before work with family segments.
- **Order of the initial interview:** nuclear family field first (symptom chronology → parental ego mass since marriage → "fix" at nodal events → projection check), then the two extended family fields.
- **The survey precedes and shapes the therapy plan.** "The family field survey is primarily for the therapist in knowing the family and how it operates, and in planning the psychotherapy."
- **Triage rule from the survey:** slow symptom development → slow nuclear buildup; rapid symptom development → explore the extended family; if the symptom is a response to the extended family, treat it as "acute" and expect restoration to be "fairly easy."
- **Preferred sequence of formats:** husband and wife together throughout (optimum) → if not possible, one family member (30–40% of hours) → in conflictual marriages typically a few months to a year with one spouse *before* joint work becomes possible.
- **[p2 correction] Bowen's own enumeration is "two main avenues," plus a third named as less effective — not "three avenues, ranked."** Verbatim: "there are two main avenues toward a higher level of 'differentiation of self'… A **third avenue is less effective**: (3) the entire process under the guidance of a supervisor who coaches from the sidelines." The two-plus-one structure matters, because the demoted third avenue is sideline coaching — the exact modality that later becomes his primary method (Ch14, Ch22). Recording it as a flat ranked list of three obscured that.
- **The avenues to higher differentiation, as enumerated:**
  1. Differentiation from one's spouse, as a cooperative effort, in the presence of a potential triangle (the therapist) who stays detached — "the 'magic' of family psychotherapy." Preconditions: the spouses "must be sufficiently involved with each other to stand the stress of 'differentiation' and sufficiently uncomfortable to motivate the effort." Sequence: "One, and then the other, moves forward in small steps until motivation stops."
  2. Start the differentiation alone under a supervisor, as a preliminary step to differentiating from the important other person — the model for family psychotherapy with one family member.
  3. "[T]he entire process under the guidance of a supervisor who coaches from the sidelines" — least effective: direct use of the triangle is lost, the process is slower, and "the chances of an impasse are greater."
- **The predictable stages after a successful "I" position** (told to the person in advance):
  1. Attack: "You are mean, selfish and vicious; you do not understand, you do not love, and you are trying to hurt the other."
  2. "When they can listen to the expected attack without reacting, a milestone will have been passed."
  3. Withdrawal: "To heck with you. I do not need you." — **"This will be the most difficult stage."** The person "might get depressed and confused and develop a whole spectrum of physical symptoms," which is "the reaction of one's psyche and soma as it cries out for the old dependence and togetherness."
  4. If they can live with the symptoms without reacting: "they can expect the other to make a new and different bid for affection on a higher level of maturity."
  5. "It is usually not many days after that before the other spouse asks to take the therapy hour, and often not many hours before they can finally work together."
- **The detriangling sequence:** initial survey estimates intensity → if mild, parents focus on their own problems immediately and the child becomes symptom-free → even in severe triangling, a "trial run" with both parents → one parent differentiates → other parent and child fuse, alternately attacking and pleading → if the "I" holds "for even a few days," automatic and permanent decrease in triangle intensity → second parent does the same → parental relationship comes "a little more to life" → another cycle with each parent separately → "still more life and zest between the parents."
- **Sick-spouse sequence:** about six months with the sick one, then some two years with both; "[a] goal is to propose 'family' early and wait until the 'self' of the sick one can operate in the presence of the other without going into dysfunction."
- **Duration for real change:** "about four years at once a week for a total of 175 to 200 hours." He is "now experimenting with less frequent appointments to reduce the total amount of time."
- **Order of change effects:** parental relationship changes first; children become symptom-free "in a few weeks or months"; changes then go "far beyond the nuclear family into the extended family system."
- **Order of opposition:** the spouses first; then families of origin, where "there are the same emotional reactions and resolutions as between themselves"; then work and social systems. "If two spouses can make the primary changes in relation to each other, it is relatively easy to deal with the other systems."
- **Timing rule on interpretation:** related events cannot be shown to the family early; wait until they are "able to 'hear.'"
- **Optimum condition for family recovery:** "It is optimum when the family system can begin a shift toward recovery with the important members of the family attending the hours."

## B4. Stated failure modes

- **The therapist becomes healer/repairman** → the family "goes into dysfunction to wait for the therapist to accomplish his work."
- **The family becomes worse under joint work** → "the 'helpless one' becoming more helpless in response to the overfunctioning of the other." Some struggle through and recover; "others would terminate."
- **Alternating between spouses** → triangling; neither works at the problem; each expects the other to do it; each justifies self to the therapist.
- **Running differentiation with both spouses at once** → "intense 'triangling.'"
- **Energy invested in the self of the therapist** → this *is* the transference; "progress will slow down or stop."
- **Showing the family the coincident-events pattern too early** → "Some families found reason to never return."
- **Bringing the other spouse in too early with a somatically dysfunctioning spouse** → "the therapy effort may terminate within a few hours." (Mechanism stated: "[t]he cells of the 'sick' spouse literally go into dysfunction in the presence of the other spouse.")
- **Trying to "treat" the healthiest member with a medical orientation** → drives him away, or makes him into a patient.
- **Adopting the differentiation principles without making them one's own** → "the effort will fail if they try them without incorporating them into 'self' as their own beliefs."
- **Third avenue (sideline coaching only, no triangle)** → slower process, greater chance of impasse.
- **Improving communication alone** → dramatic short-term shifts and exhilaration, but no resolution of underlying problems.
- **Keeping the symptomatic child in the sessions** → symptoms disappear, families terminate pleased, and "[t]here was no basic change in the pattern of the parental relationship."
- **Motivation collapse after symptom relief** → in the 25–50 range, "[m]ost in this range will lose motivation when the emotional equilibrium is restored and symptoms disappear." In the 0–25 range, once symptom relief and a dependent attachment are gained, "they are satisfied with the result."
- **Well-spouse consultations** → "remarkable symptom relief with a few appointments, but these people are not motivated for more than symptom relief."
- **Cyclical accuse/excuse in joint sessions** → nonproductive; consumes time.
- **Seeking allies against the emotional system** → fusion with the allies, harder to leave than the original.
- **The therapist failing to define himself** → the family stalls: "When the family goes slowly at defining self, I begin to wonder if there is some vague ambiguous area of importance about which I failed to define myself."
- **Debating terminology with colleagues** → "nonproductive cyclical debates that consumed both time and energy"; his response was to change vocabulary rather than argue.
- **Static psychiatric terminology** → "impaired" the effort to think of the family as a fluid, ever-changing functional system; "the loose use of psychiatric terms, such as 'depressed,' 'hysterical,' and 'compulsive,' interfered with accurate description and communication."

## B5. What the technique assumes about the family

*Each rule of technique, converted into the claim about family functioning it presupposes.*

1. **Refusing to diagnose anyone assumes** that pathology is not located in a person. A diagnosis is a move in the family's own projection process ("fixed there with a diagnosis"), so a therapist who issues one is completing the family's mechanism rather than observing it. → *Symptom location is an output of system dynamics, not a property of the individual.*
2. **Refusing the "therapist"/"healer" role assumes** that the family's functioning level is not fixed but reallocatable, and that an outsider who accepts responsibility for repair will be absorbed as an overfunctioner — at which point the family will *drop* into dysfunction to complete the reciprocity. → *Overfunction–dysfunction reciprocity operates across the family boundary and includes professionals.*
3. **Taking the "consultant"/"supervisor" role and aiming to make the family "system experts" assumes** that the family can carry out its own regulation once it can see the pattern, and that the capacity must survive the therapist's absence and the *next* stress.
4. **Staying emotionally detached as the third point assumes** the detached-third law: a two-person tension resolves automatically inside a three-person system whose third member does not take sides. The therapist is therefore *functionally a triangle vertex*, not an external agent. → *A triangle with one non-reactive vertex is a stable dissipative structure.*
5. **Never seeing spouses alternately assumes** that the therapist becomes a competed-for resource, that each spouse's account is a bid for alliance, and that a therapist who alternates *is* the third leg of a triangle that both are working — which removes the pressure that would otherwise force each to work on self.
6. **Refusing to run differentiation with both spouses simultaneously assumes** that the differentiating move is inherently destabilising to the other, and that two simultaneous destabilisations have nowhere to go but into the therapist.
7. **Taking "I" stands only as "what I will do and will not do," never "what is best," assumes** that families change by *observing* a differentiated self, not by receiving instructions — and that prescriptive advice is itself a fusion move (taking responsibility for another's course).
8. **The rule that the therapist must not ask what he does not do assumes** the therapist's own differentiation level is an active variable in the family's outcome — the ceiling on what the family can do is partly set by the therapist's own functioning.
9. **Diagnosing his own failure to define self when the family stalls assumes** that stalls in the family are *coupled to* the therapist's ambiguity, i.e. the therapist–family system has the same equilibrium mechanics as the family.
10. **Requiring the person to make the principles "their own" assumes** that borrowed convictions function as pseudo-self and cannot bear the opposition that differentiation provokes — the person would be a "transilient personality" using the therapist as outside authority.
11. **Assigning the "research observer" role and spending most of each hour on their self-report assumes** that observing self is the operative therapeutic act, and that the intellectual system can be strengthened relative to the feeling system by deliberate exercise.
12. **Forecasting the attack → withdrawal → new bid sequence assumes** that the family's counter-move to differentiation is *stereotyped and predictable*, i.e. the togetherness force has a characteristic response curve rather than an idiosyncratic one — and that knowing the curve in advance lets a person tolerate the trough without reacting.
13. **Warning that depression, confusion and physical symptoms come in the withdrawal stage assumes** that these symptoms are the *soma's* reaction to loss of fusion, not evidence of a new illness — and therefore that they must be lived with rather than treated.
14. **The rule that a stand held "for even a few days" produces a permanent decrease in triangle intensity assumes** the system has hysteresis: change in one vertex is not fully reversible, so short-duration differentiation buys durable structural change.
15. **Working with the overfunctioner rather than the dysfunctioner assumes** the reciprocity is symmetric in its arithmetic but asymmetric in its accessibility — the same total can be reduced from either end, but only one end has the free energy to move.
16. **Working with one family member at all assumes** that a single member's level change propagates through the whole ego mass — "if it is possible to get some differentiation started in one family member, it can loosen up the entire family system."
17. **Asking to see "the healthiest, best-integrated one" assumes** that leverage over a system sits with its highest-functioning member, and that naming him "healthiest" *itself* disrupts the family's diagnostic assignment ("You are the sick one who needs a psychiatrist").
18. **Sending the symptomatic child home assumes** the child's symptom is a downstream expression of the parental relationship, so the child is not a necessary participant in his own cure — a claim tested and supported by children who were never seen becoming symptom-free.
19. **Doing a "trial run" with both parents even in severe triangling assumes** that the intensity of a projection process is not readable from the presenting symptom alone and must be probed behaviourally.
20. **Detriangling by having *one parent at a time* differentiate from the child assumes** that a triangle cannot be dissolved by joint action, only by sequential unilateral moves, and that the remaining pair will predictably intensify before releasing.
21. **Keeping the other spouse out until the sick one can function in his presence assumes** that dysfunction is elicited *in relationship*, at the cellular level — the sick spouse's functioning level is a function of who is in the room.
22. **Relating to the well spouse "about his problems with the sick one" assumes** that the well one's overfunctioning is the accessible half of the pair and that engaging the sick side would recruit the therapist into it.
23. **The survey's insistence on exact dates across two-plus generations assumes** that the family's real causal structure is a *timeline of coupled events across fields*, recoverable arithmetically even though the family's own account has been systematically degraded.
24. **The assumption behind persistently questioning vague dates** is that vagueness is not memory failure but an active function of the emotional system: "[t]he family emotional system operates always to obscure and misremember and to treat such events as coincidental."
25. **Asking specifically about the mother's fantasy system before and after a birth assumes** the projection process is detectable at onset in the mother's internal worry-state, and that its duration since pregnancy indexes severity.
26. **Timing the therapy plan off the speed of symptom onset assumes** that acute onset implicates the extended field and slow onset implicates nuclear buildup — i.e. that time-course carries information about which field the load came from.
27. **Surveying grandparents' health, occupation and marriage, and each sibling's life course and contact frequency, assumes** that a person's position and involvement in his family of origin predicts how he will operate in his marriage *and in therapy*: "a life style developed in the family of origin will operate in the nuclear family and also in family psychotherapy."
28. **Withholding the coincident-events picture from the family assumes** that a family confronted with evidence of its own systemic coupling before it can absorb it will *leave* — the interpretation is not merely premature, it is repelled by the same mechanism that produced the misremembering.
29. **Recording family statements as "one family member said another was an alcoholic" assumes** that a family's descriptions of its members are reliable data about the *relationship system* and unreliable data about the *person*.
30. **Believing the family will "later applaud" a differentiation stand assumes** the opposition is a homeostatic reflex, not a considered position — and therefore is time-limited if not reinforced by capitulation.
31. **Working with both spouses as the optimum assumes** the marital dyad is where the family's load is set, so change there redistributes load everywhere else — including into both extended families.
32. **Expecting four years assumes** that basic level (as against functional level) moves slowly, and that fast symptomatic improvement is a functional-level change that reverts.
33. **Telling new families about earlier families' successes and failures assumes** the courses are repeatable across families — "[t]he most successful families followed remarkably consistent courses."
34. **Treating every family as a "research family" assumes** an observational, non-therapeutic stance produces better outcomes than a therapeutic one, and that the family's learning and the therapist's learning are mutually reinforcing.
35. **Banning psychiatric jargon assumes** that static individual-diagnostic language actively prevents perception of a fluid functional system — vocabulary constrains what can be observed.
36. **[p2] Ranking sideline coaching last assumes** that the therapist's *physical co-presence as an available triangle vertex* carries most of the method's power — "[d]irect use of the 'triangle' is lost" is given as the reason it is slower and impasse-prone. → *Efficacy is a property of the coach's structural availability, not of the information he transmits.* This is the assumption Bowen later abandons, and it is the one this model's costless external coach most depends on.
37. **[p2] Asking the mother about her fantasy system "before and after the birth" and treating duration-since-pregnancy as severity assumes** that the projection process has a *start date* that predates any observable behaviour in the child, and that its severity integrates over elapsed time rather than tracking current intensity.
38. **[p2] Collecting occupation, residence, home purchase and occupational success/failure in the survey assumes** these are *perturbations of the emotional field*, not resources — they enter as timed events that shift functioning, never as stocks a person can spend.

# CROSS-CUTTING

## X1. Contradictions and revisions

- **"Family psychotherapy" redefined mid-career.** Originally an attendance criterion ("two or more family members were seen together," with "individual psychotherapy" for one person, an explicit "'either-or' distinction"). Now an *orientation* criterion: "a theoretical system that 'thinks' in terms of family and works toward improving the family system is family psychotherapy" — so about one-third of his "family" hours are with one person. He notes the reason for the earlier split was his own limitation: "I had not dealt with my own emotional functioning sufficiently nor developed techniques to avoid a transference."
- **Abandonment of the whole-family-with-child format.** He formerly saw both parents and the symptomatic child together for a year or more with good symptomatic results and no basic change; he now sends the child home. This supersedes his own earlier practice by his own account.
- **Change in the content of the work with couples.** "In the past I stressed the communication of feelings and the analysis of the unconscious through dreams. More recently, it has been a process of watching the step by step process of externalizing and separating out their fantasy, feeling, thinking systems."
- **The triangle is deliberately kept out of the theory proper.** He names *three* major theoretical concepts, then introduces triangles separately: "It was not included with the other concepts because it has more to do with therapy than the basic theory" — while simultaneously calling the triangle "[t]he basic building block of any emotional system." The chapter thus asserts both that the triangle is foundational and that it is not one of the theory's concepts.
- **The central term is conceded to be inaccurate.** "The term undifferentiated family ego mass has been more utilitarian than accurate. Precisely defined, the four words do not belong together."
- **"Emotional illness" conceded to be the wrong label.** "[T]he family theory of emotional illness. It would be more accurate to say 'family dysfunction.'"
- **Direction of causation in overfunction–dysfunction reverses between acute and chronic.** The overfunctioner's own account ("necessary to compensate for the poor functioning of the other") is "valid in the case of temporary illness" but wrong in chronic states, where "the dysfunction appears later to compensate for overfunction in the other." He then declines to settle it: "However it develops, the overfunction-dysfunction is a reciprocating mechanism."
- **Emotional distance is called "[t]he most common mechanism"** spouses use to manage fusion, yet it is not one of the three enumerated within-family mechanisms.
- **Deliberate abandonment of theoretical integration.** He worked toward correlating family concepts with psychoanalytic theory in the early years, then "elected to describe the family phenomenon in terms that did not stir up debates... and to leave integration of individual and family concepts for some future generation."
- **He retains "family psychotherapy" despite acknowledging inaccuracies** in it, "as the best working compromise between the theory and the practice."
- **Toman's profiles are adopted whole and then qualified**: accurate especially mid-scale, but Toman worked on "normal" families, estimated no other variables, and did not account for the projection-altered child.
- **Terminology reform vs. communicability.** He banned jargon internally for accuracy, then reintroduced familiar terms sparingly because "[i]t is difficult to communicate with colleagues without using familiar terms."
- **[p2] He forbids the repairman role and then adopts the engineer role.** B2 records "[d]o not become a 'healer' or 'repairman'"; the same chapter says "the therapist became a kind of 'expert' in understanding family systems and an 'engineer' in helping the family restore itself to functioning equilibrium." He does not reconcile these, and the distinction he presumably intends — repairing the *person* versus engineering the *system* — is never stated.
- **[p2] "Quantitative measures" asserted, then withdrawn in practice.** "The degree of the problem between the spouses can be assigned quantitative measures" stands two paragraphs from "approximate levels can be established only after having awareness of the particular variables most operative over a period of time for a given family" and from the flat disclaimer that the scale figures are "strictly schematic." The chapter claims measurability and supplies no measure.

## X2. Hedges and uncertainty

- **Explicit refusal of causal inference on coincident events:** "In theoretical thinking, I have never been willing to postulate causality or go beyond noting that such events have a striking time sequence."
- **Speculation flagged as such:** about why these sequences are denied — "I believe it may have to do with man's denial of dependence on his fellow man."
- **The scale figures are schematic.** "[T]he scale level figures used in the preceding examples are to illustrate the broad principles"; "[t]he levels in the multigenerational concept are strictly schematic and for illustrative purposes only."
- **Levels are hard to estimate at all in the lower half:** shifts are "so responsive to such a variety of hour-to-hour and week-to-week shifts, through good years and bad, that approximate levels can be established only after having awareness of the particular variables most operative over a period of time for a given family."
- **The upper quarter is unobserved.** "People in the upper quarter of the scale (75 to 100) are those I have never seen in my clinical work and that I rarely meet in social and professional relationships"; "it is essentially impossible for anyone to have all the characteristics I would assign to 100."
- **The multigenerational maximum-impairment pattern is acknowledged impossible as literally described:** "It would be essentially impossible for this pattern to occur generation after generation, but it does illustrate the pattern"; and "[t]o have two children, one at 20 and another at 45, is hardly probable."
- **Correlating the scale with diagnostic categories is disclaimed:** "This scale has nothing to do with diagnostic categories"; "[i]t is misleading to correlate this scale with clinical categories."
- **No concept of normal:** "This theory does not have a concept of 'normal.'"
- **Deliberate refusal to specify the system type:** "For the moment I shall not attempt to say what kind of system. There is no single word or term that would be accurate without further qualification, and qualification would distort the system concept."
- **Inadequacy of available vocabulary:** "There is something wrong with any single term that has been used" to describe what family members do.
- **Acknowledged bias:** "any attempt to explain or describe the family movement will represent the bias and viewpoint of the author."
- **Communication difficulty admitted repeatedly:** "It has been difficult to communicate the notion of avoiding a transference and 'family' psychotherapy with only one family member"; "Communication of this idea has been difficult. I have used several different concepts in trying to write about the idea."
- **Detriangling under-specified:** "This is too complex for brief discussion."
- **Four years is a belief, not a finding:** "I believe it might require this amount of time for significant differentiation of self." And he is actively experimenting with reducing it.
- **Ongoing modification acknowledged:** "both the theory and therapy have been constantly modified."
- **Unfalsified assumptions flagged as assumptions:** "However valid the ideas behind the selection of these concepts, they did play a major part in the choice of concepts."
- **He does not know why cutoff families behave as they do**, only that the pattern is "so common that I have called these exploding and cohesive families."
- **Prognostic hedging:** the 25–30 range is "a situation of possibility but low probability."
- **[p2] No external validation of any kind is offered, anywhere in the chapter.** There is no instrument, no rating procedure, no second rater, no criterion measure, and no comparison group. The only estimation procedures named are internal and clinical: "[f]unctioning within the ego mass can be estimated with a few questions about the stress areas"; approximate scale levels "can be established only after having awareness of the particular variables most operative over a period of time for a given family." The only check-against-something-else in the chapter is his own contemporaneous case notes used to contradict a family's account of a date ("[t]he husband's memory and my notes were accurate"), and one weak predict-then-verify step: sibling position "plus the general level of family functioning, makes it possible to postulate a reasonably accurate personality profile **to be checked later**" — with no report of any such check's outcome. The multigenerational concept's evidential base is stated as a corpus size only: "three to four generations on approximately 100 families, and ten or more generations on eight families."
- **[p2] Source-text corruption, flagged rather than smoothed.** In the closeness-cycle passage the digitised text reads "each can **refuse** into a similar emotional involvement with another family member" — evidently an OCR corruption. Pass 1 silently rendered it as "retreat." The word is not recoverable from this file and the sentence should be treated as unquotable.

## X3. Unprompted observations

- **A scale with no normal.** The whole of human functioning on one continuum, with no privileged midpoint — a design decision he justifies by the failure of attempts to establish emotional "normals" where physical ones were easy.
- **Function is dyadic arithmetic, not individual property.** The 55/15 marriage with a basic level of 35 says a person's observed functioning is a *sum* of his own level and what he has borrowed — so measured function is not a person attribute at all.
- **Overfunctioning has its own decompensation curve.** The athlete/heart-failure tachycardia analogy: overfunction is not simply "good" — it, too, has a compensated and a decompensated range.
- **"Rejection" as a normal homeostatic operation** running constantly and usually unmentioned; and the inversion that when rejection is general, "the one who claims 'rejection' is usually more rejecting of the other."
- **Label propagation across generations.** A wife's label of her husband was accepted by children and transmitted to grandchildren, and functioned as fact for two generations of descendants.
- **The extrasensory / intrapsychic-contact claim scaled to fusion:** "[t]he more intense the ego fusions, the higher the incidence of being in touch with the intrapsychic of the other."
- **The observation loop between therapist and family:** "the more a therapist learns about a family, the more the family learns about itself; and the more the family learns, the more the therapist learns, in a cycle which continues."
- **Research families outperformed therapy families** — a finding about *stance* rather than technique, which he then generalised into making all families research families.
- **Some families restored functioning with almost no intervention**, purely under observation, and the successful ones "followed remarkably consistent courses."
- **Detachment from individual theory and perception of family patterns are mutually reinforcing:** "the more one observes families, the easier it is to detach from the narrow conceptual boundaries of individual theory; and the more one detaches from individual theory, the easier it is to see family patterns."
- **The phantom-family perception.** After thousands of hours, "it became increasingly impossible to see a single person without 'seeing' his total family sitting like phantoms alongside him" — the perceptual shift that made one-person family psychotherapy possible.
- **Rigidity of belief is an index of differentiation** — dogma, superstition, and inability to discard outmoded ideas load on the low end; and low-scale people borrow *external* authorities ("Science has shown . . .") in place of "I believe."
- **A conviction fused with feeling becomes "a cause."**
- **A "no-self" can be a *better* employee**: "[a] 'no-self' who is sufficiently adept at pleasing his boss might be considered a better employee than if he had some 'self.'" Low differentiation is not uniformly disadvantageous in every environment.
- **The socioeconomic remark:** the 0–25 group "includes the 'dead enders' of society, many of the lower socioeconomic group, and those from higher socioeconomic groups with intense ego fusions."
- **The conflictual marriage is stable** *because of* the intensity of investment — an anti-intuitive stability claim: conflict is a preservative.
- **Success at work in the 25–50 range is set by approval from superiors and the relationship system**, "than the inherent value of their work."
- **The high-scale person's relationships are described operationally:** he can engage intense relationships without a "need" that impairs functioning, and "[t]he 'other' in such a relationship does not feel 'used.'"
- **Nonrelatives get pulled into the ego mass** — agencies, clinics, schools, and courts become participants under sufficient stress. The family boundary is not the household.
- **The therapist's self-definition is a therapeutic instrument** whose absence is diagnosable in the family's rate of progress.
- **A social-systems generalisation** appears in passing: seeking an ally or minority group within a larger system produces a new oneness harder to leave than the original.
- **[p2] A symptom-free life at the bottom of the scale.** The most counter-intuitive claim in the scale section, and pass 1 missed it entirely: some people in the 0–25 band "maintain a sufficient system of dependent attachments to function through life without symptoms." Level and symptom are separate outputs *at every point on the scale*, not only in the middle — which means a model that renders low differentiation as visible impairment is wrong at the bottom as well as the top.
- **[p2] The survey's own therapeutic side effect** is named as "[a] secondary dividend" — the family's "beginning intellectual awareness of related events." Data collection is not neutral to the system it measures.
- **[p2] "Both long for closeness but both are 'allergic' to it."** An explicit approach–avoidance formulation for the pre-marital pair, which the model's monotone attraction/aversion terms cannot express.

## P1. Corrections to pass 1

**1. An inferred quantity was presented as a finding — and it is arithmetically wrong.**
A3 #32 stated the multigenerational level "descends about 15 points per generation in the schematic
(50 → 35 → 20 → 10/15)." The chapter states no rate. Its steps are −15, −15, then −10 *and* −5, and
the third-generation parent pair is never assigned a level at all (the 20 marries "a spouse whose
'needs' for another are as great as his"). Worse, pass 1 omitted the two sentences that cap the whole
schematic: "[i]n the average situation the immaturity would progress at a much slower rate," and "in
the average family the upward progression is much slower than illustrated in this example." A
calibration target was manufactured from an illustration Bowen explicitly bounded. Corrected in A3
and A4.

**2. Later theory was read back into a chapter that predates it.** Pass 1 was a cold read and should
have carried none of this. It carried four instances:
- A5 wrote "the **four** *within*-family mechanisms are here only three." The chapter has no fourth
  mechanism and never suggests one; "four" comes from Ch16/Ch18, a decade later.
- A5 said "undifferentiated family ego mass" carries work "later split among fusion, nuclear family
  emotional system, and family projection" — all later vocabulary.
- A3 #31 and M use **"cut-off" / "cutoff"** as if it were the chapter's term. It is not; the word does
  not occur. The chapter says a spouse who "separates from his family of origin does not resolve the
  emotional attachment," and names the family types "exploding" and "cohesive."
- M says "cutoff is a relationship state with suppressed-but-nonzero coupling" — a model claim
  attributed to a chapter that has no such concept.
The comparative content is correct; it belongs here, not in Stream A. Flagged in place.

**3. "The three avenues to higher differentiation, ranked" mis-states Bowen's own enumeration**, and
the mis-statement hides the single most important evolution datum in the chapter. He writes: "there
are **two main avenues**… A **third avenue is less effective**: (3) the entire process under the
guidance of a supervisor who **coaches from the sidelines**." Sideline coaching — the method that
becomes his primary modality in Ch14 and the entire subject of Ch22 — is here explicitly the worst of
three, because "[d]irect use of the 'triangle' is lost." Flattening it into a ranked list of three
lost the two-plus-one structure and the demotion. Corrected in B3; a new B5 #36 records the
assumption underneath it.

- Family assigns diagnoses/characteristics, which become "an operational fact" if accepted; labels propagate across generations → **`unmodelled`** → labels as heritable objects with three responses (resist / alternate / accept) and an effect on the labelled person's functioning.
- Nonrelatives, agencies, schools and courts absorbed into the ego mass under stress → **`unmodelled`** → the family boundary should be permeable and stress-dependent.
- Extrasensory / intrapsychic contact and "gut reaction" scaling with fusion → **`unmodelled`** → a fusion-dependent information channel independent of communication.
- Emotional / feeling / intellectual three-layer stack; "I feel" vs "I believe" → **`unmodelled`** → currently there is no distinction between a person's feeling output and reasoned output; the differentiation scale largely *is* the ratio between them.
- Communication improvement gives fast, dramatic, non-durable change → `test` → a distractor mechanism that must move functional level and *not* basic level.
- Conflictual marriage is enduring *because of* the investment; thinking-time in the other exceeds calm marriages → **`unmodelled`** → relationship stability should rise with conflict intensity in this configuration, which is the opposite of a naive model.
- "Fraudulent" emotional contract at marriage onset → `Relationship` → an initial condition where each pledges a high fraction of self, making functioning other-dependent and blame automatic.
- Adaptiveness reaching "cell to cell"; the adaptive spouse usually the more passive → `Person` → an `adaptiveness` trait that routes symptom expression toward the physical channel.
- Low differentiation → high incidence of physical illness → `Person` → physical-symptom probability as a decreasing function of basic level.
- Rejection as a constant, normal equilibrium mechanism; the claimant of rejection is usually the more rejecting → `Relationship` / `test` → adversarial test: self-reported rejection must not be a valid measure of received rejection.
- Slow onset ⇒ nuclear buildup; fast onset ⇒ extended-family disturbance and easy restoration → `coach` / `test` → the coach's triage rule; testable against generated histories.
- Reporting family statements as "one family member said . . ." rather than as fact → `coach` → the coach must never write a family claim into the model as ground truth.
- No concept of "normal" in the theory → `test` → guard that no code path treats a mid-scale value as a default/healthy baseline.
