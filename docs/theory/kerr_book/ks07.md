## ⭐⭐⭐ KS07.1 — **The discriminator case.** One relationship, two improvements, one functional and one basic `[T]` `[M]`

This chapter runs a single clinical case from courtship to resolution and **explicitly labels which changes were functional and which were basic.** It is the acceptance-test scenario `M11.C.18` asks for, already worked, by the source.

**Improvement 1 — functional.** The husband's first cousin visits town for a week; they lunch repeatedly and talk by phone most nights.

> "the wife described a **dramatic improvement in her depression and bowel symptoms.** Her endocrinologist told her… that **her blood tests related to the diabetes had also significantly improved.** She was alone a lot during that week… and described lying on her bed and feeling like a great pressure had been lifted. She explained, '**I feel free to be my own person, back in control of me.**'"

> "**It did not last long after the cousin left town. The regression again deepened, and her symptoms returned.**"

**Improvement 2 — basic.** In therapy the wife grasps the reciprocity, "**got freer of self-blame and blaming her husband**", and takes a position: "**If you have to leave to seek happiness, I will have to accept it. I don't want that, but I can accept it.**"

> "The husband responded in a predictable way when someone effectively defines a 'self.' He repeatedly said, '**You're wrong, change back; if you don't these are the consequences.**'"

After "**nearly a week of the husband's anxiety at its peak and the wife holding her own**":

> "the husband blurted out, '**You are acting disgustingly mature.**' He laughed!"

And the labelling, in Kerr's own words:

> "**This was a basic change brought about by the wife developing a fraction more solid self; it was not like the previous improvement, a functional shift brought about by the husband's focus on his cousin.**"

*Model consequence:* **the single most valuable fixture in the book.** Two symptom remissions of comparable magnitude in the same dyad, one produced by the automatic channel (anxiety displaced onto a third party — a `TRIANGLE`) and one by the self-directed channel (`I-POSITION`), with the source stating which is which and why.

Everything `M11.C.18` needs is here: the functional improvement **reverted on removal of the external condition**; the basic one **persisted and changed the relationship's balance**. And the distinguishing feature is not the size of the symptom change — the cousin week produced measurable laboratory improvement — but **what happens when the supporting condition is withdrawn.** That is `M1.A.4a`'s estimator doing exactly its job.

Note also the ladder appears **verbatim** — "You're wrong, change back; if you don't these are the consequences" — confirming `M5.E.1` from a fifth source, with a **one-week** duration at peak before it broke (`M5.E.6`, and compare `S2.6`'s "months" — this is the acute reaction, not the system's re-equilibration).

## ⭐⭐ KS07.2 — The **"whisper of nature"**: split a discrepant pair and the discrepancy reverses `[T]` `[D]`

> "if two spouses with significantly discrepant functioning **split up, the dysfunctional one often becomes more functional and the previously higher functioning or overfunctioning spouse becomes less functional, occasionally to the point of serious dysfunction.** It does not always happen this way, but its occurrence **strongly suggests that the spouses' basic levels of 'self' were not different.** I call this phenomenon a '**whisper of nature**,' meaning that it reveals an underlying process in nature that is often difficult to see if it is less extreme."

The companion observation:

> "usually the partners **did not begin their relationship having such a discrepancy** in their functioning; **the discrepancy developed over time**… the higher the level of chronic anxiety, **the greater the divergence in functioning.**"

*Model consequence:* **a clean, falsifiable, two-arm acceptance criterion, and it tests the model's core claim directly.** Construct a dominant-adaptive pair at matched `basic_level`, run it until functional levels diverge, then dissolve the tie: the underfunctioner's `functional_level` **MUST** rise and the overfunctioner's **MUST** fall, converging toward their common basic level. If the model cannot produce this, `M6.I.4`'s pseudo-self transfer is not doing its work.

It also gives `M2.A.0c` its strongest support: divergence is **generated by the relationship**, not brought to it, and its magnitude scales with chronic anxiety. Add to `M11.C` — and note it is the one criterion that would make `S2.13`'s ±1 tolerance observable, since the initial gap must be small enough that the *developed* divergence dominates it.

## ⭐⭐ KS07.3 — **Selfish and unselfish are the two axes**, named by Kerr and warned about `[T]`

> "It is common in these types of situations for the husband's behaviors to be depicted as '**selfish**' and the wife's as '**unselfish**.' The wife views it that way, and outsiders usually do too. **Bowen theory views selfish and unselfish as emotionally based postures people take in relationships in an effort to satisfy togetherness needs. Both postures reflect the inability to maintain 'self' in the relationship.** In this case, **one manifests it by pressuring the other, the husband encroaching on the wife's emotional boundaries, and the other manifests it by trying to please, molding her words and actions to appease him.**"

> "**If the selfish and unselfish terms are thought of as character traits rather than as polar opposite positions produced by a reciprocal process, it is almost impossible not to view the 'unselfish' partner as a victim and the 'selfish' partner as the culprit.**"

*Model consequence:* **`M1.A.9a` confirmed in the exact vocabulary the user supplied from Kerr** — *be for self without being selfish; be for other without being selfless.* Here the two failures are named and mapped:

| Axis | Kerr's term | Behaviour in this case |
|---|---|---|
| Outward impingement | "**selfish**" | "encroaching on the wife's emotional boundaries" |
| Inward impingement | "**unselfish**" | "trying to please, molding her words and actions to appease him" |

Both are **failures of the same kind**, and the second quote states precisely why a **scalar** counterfeit score is dangerous rather than merely imprecise: it collapses into victim-and-culprit. That is `M5.F.2b`'s requirement to report *which axis failed*, and it is also a `M11.F` framing requirement.

## ⭐ KS07.4 — Investment measures **dependence**, not interest `[T]`

> "**the amount of emotional/psychic energy invested in the relationship does not equate with a deep, genuine, unselfish, and respectful interest** people can have in each other. In contrast, **it equates with the level of dependence each person's sense of well-being has on the other's real and imagined investment** in the other; **the more energy invested in the relationship, the more people are 'completing' themselves by borrowing pseudo-self from the other.**"

*Model consequence:* `M1.B.8`'s valence-blind `investment` confirmed, and **linked directly to pseudo-self borrowing** — high investment *is* high mutual borrowing. That closes a gap: `M1.B.8` (investment) and `M6.I.4` (pseudo-self transfer) are currently separate quantities with no stated relationship. They should be one.

Note "**real and imagined**" again (third occurrence, after `KS01.7` and `KS04.7`): the dependence is on the *perceived* investment of the other.

## ⭐ KS07.5 — Which pattern is active determines **how** functional levels diverge `[T]` `[M]`

> "The borrowing and trading of pseudo-self **can be equal**, resulting in **each person's functional level being enhanced and similar.**"

> "an increase in chronic anxiety… **propel a disparity in functional levels if the predominant pattern is dominant-deferential.** If the predominant pattern is **emotional conflict**, the chronic anxiety predominantly plays out in conflict, and **each partner's functional level is maintained reasonably well; but their relationship is full of turmoil.** If the pattern is a **triangle with focus on a child**, the parents overfunction and the child underfunctions. **Sometimes with a child-focused pattern the child functions fairly well for a time but eventually dysfunctions. This often happens when the child leaves home.**"

*Model consequence:* **a per-pattern signature on the functional-level distribution**, which is directly assertable:

| Active pattern | Functional-level outcome |
|---|---|
| Equal borrowing | both **raised**, similar |
| Dominant-deferential | **diverge** |
| Emotional conflict | both **maintained**, high turmoil |
| Child triangle | parents up, child down — **delayed**, often at launch |

`M11.C` candidate covering all four arms at once. The delayed-onset case is `KS04.7`'s loop and matches the Ch 4 clinical vignette (the daughter's psychotic break in her first year at college), so it now has two independent instances.

## ⭐ KS07.6 — Internal versus external stressors, and isolation as a stressor `[T]`

> "**internal stressors**: Each partner's reactivity to **real and imagined verbal and nonverbal cues perceived in the other related to attention, approval, expectations, and distress**, a reactivity that **varies with basic level of differentiation**."

> "**External stressors** are ones brought into the relationship from sources outside… such as work issues."

> "Internal and external stressors are **interrelated**… one partner's worry and preoccupation with a work issue may result in the other's **feeling inadequately attended to**, thus setting off an escalating interactive process."

In the case: "the couple's **emotional isolation was an important external stressor**." Its sources — the husband's possessiveness "**manifested in him pushing his wife to spend less time with her friends. She reluctantly complied, which is her part in the process.** Furthermore, **neither spouse had extended family members with whom they maintained close contact.**"

*Model consequence:* three things.

1. **`KS01.7`'s four cues confirmed as a closed set** — attention, approval, expectations, distress — now explicitly as the *internal* stressor channel with gain varying on basic level. Second appearance, identical list. Safe to treat as the appraisal decomposition.
2. **The external→internal conversion path is named**: an external stressor becomes an internal one by consuming the partner's attention. That is `M1.A.10`'s life-energy budget acting as the coupling, and it is how `M4.A`'s exogenous events should reach the tie.
3. **Isolation is a stressor with two named sources** — one *produced* by the pattern (possessiveness cutting off friends) and one *inherited* (no extended-family contact). This is `KS05.15`'s protective external-tie term, and the first source shows the system **actively destroys its own protection**. `M5.B.3a`'s cutoff floor and `M1.A.12`'s involvement weight both bear on it.

## KS07.7 — Assortative pairing, with de Waal's similarity list `[T]`

> "**people with the same basic level of differentiation predictably select one another as partners** for an intimate relationship."

De Waal (1996) on the similarity principle across species; his human list — "**age, socioeconomic status, political preference, religion, ethnic background, IQ level, education, physical attractiveness, and height.**"

> "**Basic level of differentiation can be added to this list.**"

> "**Exceptions occur rarely in marriages — there are not nearly as many exceptions as marital partners often claim!**"

> "Basic levels of differentiation **also influence the development of close friendships but somewhat less precisely.**"

*Model consequence:* `M2.A.0c` confirmed for a third time, with two refinements. Pairing is **strict for spouses, looser for friendships** — so the model may apply `S2.13`'s ±1 tolerance to marriage and a wider one to non-family ties. And Kerr states the methodological obstacle himself: "**it is difficult to research this idea because of the difficulty of being more precise about basic levels… than just making estimates**", compounded because pseudo-self trading makes one partner *look* more mature. That is `A7` again, and it is why the model's own estimator must be load- and breadth-conditioned.

## KS07.8 — Equal investment at the start `[T]`

> "**The partnering process begins with each person investing an equal amount of emotional/psychic energy in the relationship.**"

> "The members of both couples **monitor each other and adjust accordingly, but the monitoring is more intense in more fused couples.**"

*Model consequence:* a construction rule for `M2.A` — tie investment initialises **symmetric**, and asymmetry is *generated*, matching `KS07.2`. And "monitoring intensity" scales with fusion, which is the mechanism behind `M4.C.1`'s conductance: fused pairs sample each other's cues more often, so more events reach appraisal.

## KS07.9 — Courtship's comfortable fit can itself be a pattern `[T]`

> "Early in the courtship period **the pattern often contributes to a comfortable fit** in that each person may be playing out a functioning position that **replicates aspects of that person's particular developmental years, such as when an older brother of a younger sister courts a younger sister of an older brother.** It **binds** any relationship-generated chronic anxiety in that **if the female in this case was less deferential and the male is bothered by it, the relationship might not take.**"

*Model consequence:* `M1.A.14`'s `sibling_position` acquires a **selection** role, not only a propensity one — complementary positions make the pair *more likely to form*. And the last clause is a real mechanism: **incompatible positions cause the pairing to fail**, so the observed population of couples is filtered. `M2.A`'s construction should reflect that filter rather than pairing at random within a level band.

Note it also means the dominant-deferential pattern can be present **from the start and comfortable**, which is consistent with `KS04.18` (patterns shift) and with `KS00.10` (driver, not type).

## KS07.10 — Two levels of relationship balance, and body regression `[T]`

> "**This is not a basic change but an anxiety-driven functional change.**" — of the post-marriage tightening.

> "Not only is the relationship in a regression, but **her bodily homeostasis is so disturbed by her level of chronic anxiety that her body is in a regression too. It is speculative at this point, but it is possible that the regression that can impair the functioning of one member of a family may obey the same natural laws that govern the regression in a body** that leads to organ and tissue dysfunction."

*Model consequence:* confirms `KS06.5` — relationships carry a basic and a functional balance — and the case labels each transition as one or the other throughout, which is what makes it usable as a fixture. The body-regression parallel is explicitly speculative and is Kerr's own conjecture; record as `[K-ext]` and **do not implement**. It becomes Ch 23's *Unidisease* proposal, so hold for that chapter.

## KS07.11 — The therapist defines the problem; they do not instruct `[T]`

> "**The therapist does not instruct the family on how to change. The therapist primarily helps the family define the problem more factually, which automatically opens up new options.**"

> "**The thinking people do between sessions propels much of the progress they make.**"

> "It usually takes **longer than six sessions** for a family leader to emerge and define a 'self,' and the process is **usually more complicated than in this case.**"

*Model consequence:* `M1.E.2a` confirmed for a fifth time, and `M1.E.7`'s landed-contact definition sharpened: what a contact delivers is a **more factual definition of the problem**, and the option-generation is automatic downstream of it. That is `KS05.9`'s mindware again.

The dose figures are worth recording against `KB07` · K07.4's "four times at most, five maybe, in two years": this case took **six sessions** and Kerr flags it as **atypically fast and simplified**. Both belong in `M10.C` as `[#]` with their hedges attached — the model should not calibrate to the fast case.
