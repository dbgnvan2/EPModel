# Ch07 — Intrafamily Dynamics in Emotional Illness

## 0. Provenance
- **Stated date / venue (from the text itself):** none given. The chapter carries no presentation venue, journal, or publication date in its body. Dating must be reconstructed from internal markers: "During the last ten years there has been a rapidly increasing interest in family treatment"; "In the early 1950s efforts were begun to involve the family in treatment"; "In the last six or eight years the number of people working with families has multiplied each year"; "My shift from 'individual' to 'family' began fifteen years ago"; the formal research "started in 1954" and "was continued for five years"; "In 1959 the research was moved to a new setting"; "For eight years I have been looking for the best way to conceptualize the idea of a 'family emotional oneness.'" The two endnotes name the settings: (1) Clinical Center, National Institute of Mental Health, Bethesda, Maryland, 1954–1959; (2) Department of Psychiatry, Georgetown University Medical Center, Washington, D.C.
- **Clinical setting and data source (who was observed, how many, over what period):** Three overlapping sources, none quantified. (a) Individual psychotherapy with schizophrenic patients and with various members of their families, beginning fifteen years before writing. (b) The formal NIMH "live-in" research study, 1954–1959: initially mothers living in residence on the ward with the patients; after one year the plan was changed to admit both parents and normal siblings to the ward, and psychotherapy was changed so that all family members attended the hours together. The study was focused on families with a schizophrenic offspring and ran five years. (c) Concurrent clinical work with outpatient families, later extended at Georgetown to "the entire range of emotional illness," including neurotic problems and "normal" families. No case counts, no outcome figures, no controls, no statistics anywhere in the chapter.
- **Genre:** both, weighted toward theory. Roughly the first quarter is field background and an intellectual autobiography of Bowen's own shift from individual to family orientation; the middle (the observations, the undifferentiated family ego mass, "The Family Theory") is the theoretical core; the closing section "Family Psychotherapy" is technique. Approximate proportion **theory 70 : technique 30**, with the autobiographical/methodological material counted as theory because it is where the observational claims are stated.

# STREAM A — How families function

## A1. Phenomena requiring explanation

- **Cyclical symbiosis, mother and patient** → observed in individual psychotherapy with schizophrenic patients and their family members. The pair could be so close they were "emotional Siamese twins," or so distant and hostile that they repelled each other. The oscillation, not either pole, is the phenomenon.
- **The symbiosis was more intense than hypothesized, and not circumscribed** → observed once mothers lived on the ward. It was "a fragment of a larger family emotional system in which fathers were as intimately involved as the mothers," fluid and shifting, capable of extending to the entire central family unit and even to nonrelatives.
- **Family relationships alternate between overcloseness and overdistance** → live-in study families.
- **Accurate mind-reading in closeness phases** → in the closeness phase one family member "could accurately know the thoughts, feelings, fantasies, and dreams of another family member." Reported as observation, not as metaphor.
- **One ego functioning for another** → the "fusion of selfs" "could involve every area of ego functioning."
- **Cross-person somatization** → "One family member could become physically ill in response to emotional stress in another family member."
- **Conflict displacement between dyads** → "Emotional conflict between two family members could disappear with the simultaneous development of conflict between two other family members." A dyad-to-dyad transfer, not a decay.
- **Re-fusion onto substitutes in the distance phase** → separated members would fuse with other family members, with members of the hospital staff, or with "other vulnerable nonrelatives." Staff are explicitly available as fusion targets.
- **Fluid shifting of strengths and weaknesses across members** → the family as "a giant jigsaw puzzle of strength and weakness with each family member holding parts of the same puzzle, and with much trading of pieces."
- **Nonrelatives can be more fused than kin** → "Live-in servants can be more emotionally fused into the family emotional system than certain blood relatives."
- **Individual therapy relieves then relapses** → "intrafamily tensions could be toned down while any involved member of the family unit was in individual psychotherapy, only to recur when treatment ended." Bowen states this was evidence at the time and "was later confirmed."
- **Graded visibility of the same phenomenon** → after the phenomenon was clearly seen at maximum intensity in schizophrenic families, "it was then possible to see it in its less intense form in families with neurotic problems, and even in 'normal' families." The phenomenon is continuous across severity, not categorical.
- **Distance from parents can mask attachment** → "The young adult who runs away from home never to see his parents again may have more basic attachment to his parents than other siblings who continue to live with their parents."
- **Quiescent but active dependence at a distance** → a grown sibling with an old unresolved dependence, living far away with little contact: "the functioning of parents or sibling is still responsive to events that change the life course of the other."
- **Some conflict stays sealed inside the marriage** → "There are conflictive marriages in which the conflict is so contained within the marriage that there is little or no emotional involvement of the children."
- **Some marriages are calm because a child is impaired** → spouses who "transmit the entire weight of the parental problem to one or more children, resulting in a marital relationship that is calm and harmonious and a major impairment in the child."
- **Chronic disability can hold a marriage together** → a marriage "might become permanently stabilized with one spouse chronically incapacitated," and "can remain emotionally harmonious as long as the disabled spouse does not recover from the physical illness."
- **Families present for help at a specific moment** → "when its own stabilizing mechanisms have failed and family efforts to solve the problem result in 'making it worse.'"
- **Differential engagement with therapy by level** → higher-identity families stay with treatment until "some kind of permanent resolution"; lower-identity families seek immediate relief, praise the therapy glowingly on slight relief, and on failure "accept the inevitable and leave the future course to chance."
- **Fused spouses manage each other's functioning instead of their own** `[p2]` → the chapter's explicit description of the default (non-differentiating) state, which pass 1 omitted entirely: family members in the ego mass "are bogged down in an emotional morass of interdependence, each too dependent on the other to risk becoming a clear self. Spouses become so involved in being the way the other wants them to be to improve the functioning of the other, and demanding that the other be different to enhance the functioning of self, that neither is responsible for self." Two directed sub-behaviours (self-alteration for the other's benefit; demand on the other for one's own benefit) with a single stated consequence.
- **Differentiating is described as a risk, gated by dependence** `[p2]` → "each too dependent on the other to *risk* becoming a clear self." The barrier to the move scales with mutual dependence, not with motivation or insight.
- **At high level the fusion of selfs does not occur at all** `[p2]` → "These spouses do not become involved in the 'fusion of selfs' that occurs in marriages of less differentiated spouses." Stated as absence, not attenuation. See X1 for the tension this creates.
- **Observational blindness in the investigator** → Bowen reports his own failure to see the larger family system for years despite the evidence being present, and generalizes it: "Man can fail to see what is in front of his eyes unless it fits into his theoretical frame of reference" (Darwin / prehistoric bones analogy).

## A2. Mechanisms proposed

- **Undifferentiated family ego mass** → the organizing mechanism. "A fused cluster of egos of individual family members, with a common ego boundary." Acts on: the whole family unit. Membership is graded (some egos completely fused, others less) and *variable with stress*: "During periods of emotional calm the family ego mass may include only a small number of the most involved family members. During periods of stress the fusion may extend itself to include multiple members of the extended family network, and even nonrelatives." Intensity is set by maturity: "The ego fusion is most intense in the least mature families," reaching "maximum intensity" where there is a schizophrenic member.
- **Per-ego, time-varying involvement — a second and distinct variability** `[p2]` → pass 1 recorded only the aggregate radius. The chapter states the per-member version separately and immediately before it: "Some egos are intensely involved in the family ego mass during periods of emotional stress and are less involved at other times." Three variabilities are therefore stated in one paragraph, and they are not the same claim: (i) *graded depth* — "Some egos are completely fused into the family ego mass and others are less fused"; (ii) *per-ego temporal modulation by stress* — the sentence above; (iii) *aggregate radius* — calm includes "only a small number of the most involved family members," stress extends "to include multiple members of the extended family network, and even nonrelatives." (ii) is the strongest of the three for modelling: it makes involvement a continuous per-agent quantity that varies with load, of which the radius is the aggregate consequence.
- **Fusion of selfs / ego fusion** → what the ego mass is made of: obliteration of the "tenuous ego boundaries" between members, so that self is lost into a common self. Acts on: any pair or set in close emotional relationship.
- **Differentiation of self** → the counter-process. "Children grow up to achieve varying levels of differentiation of self from the undifferentiated family ego mass." Its product is "a contained emotional unit" able to "maintain his ego boundaries under stress without becoming involved in emotional fusions with others" and to be close to others "without fusing into new emotional onenesses."
- **Level-matched mate selection** → "People select spouses who have identical basic levels of differentiation of self." Acts on: pair formation. `[p2]` **Pass 1's gloss "it is what makes the level heritable across the marriage boundary" is an anachronism and is withdrawn.** The chapter states no rule by which a parent's level determines a child's; it says only that siblings from one family land at different levels ("their more differentiated siblings"; "the other children are usually more differentiated") and does not say what sets the difference. Nothing in this chapter licenses a generational inheritance rule.
- **Mutual functioning-management as the fused default `[p2]`** → the operating policy of members inside the ego mass: alter self to be as the other wants "to improve the functioning of the other," and demand the other change "to enhance the functioning of self," with the result that "neither is responsible for self." Acts on: any pair inside the mass. This is the exact inverse, term for term, of the two-part differentiating move Bowen names two sentences later, and it supplies the baseline behaviour the move has to displace.
- **Failure to grow away / pseudo-separation** → in low-differentiation people, the childhood process of "growing away" from parents does not start; they remain attached, then after adolescence "tear themselves away" to establish "pseudo-selfs" with a "pseudo-separation." Two implementations named: denial while still living at home, or reinforcement by physical distance.
- **Pseudo-self** → the functional-but-borrowed self that permits life adjustment without differentiation. It is what is merged in marriage: "the pseudo-selfs merge into a common self."
- **Vulnerability confined to the close relationship** → "The Achilles' heel of these people is the close emotional relationship." They can function in business and professional relationships "as long as they keep their relationships casual and brief." Acts on: relationship *type*, not on the person globally.
- **Marital re-fusion** → at marriage or at the establishment of "a living-together interdependence," the new husband and wife fuse into a new family ego mass, "a replica of the former emotional fusions with their parents." "The lower the level of differentiation of self, the more intense the fusion."
- **Closeness–distance regulation driven by fusion anxiety** → "Both long for emotional closeness but when they are close, the pseudo-selfs merge into a common self... and with loss of self to the common self. To avoid the anxiety of fusion, they keep sufficient emotional distance between themselves for each to function with as much pseudo-self as possible." This is an explicit negative-feedback controller: approach drive vs. loss-of-self cost, with distance as the regulator.
- **Ordered determinants of the family's course** → "the pattern and the future course of the new family ego mass are determined first by the dynamics between the two spouses, second by the maintenance of relationships to those outside the new family ego mass, and third by the intensity of the ego fusion." An explicit ranking of three causal inputs.
- **Ego-strength trading within the dyad** → "The dynamics between the new husband and wife are determined by the way they fight for, or share, the ego strength available to them." They enter "with equal levels of 'self,'" fuse into a common self, "and thereafter one spouse usually functions with more than an equal share of the available ego strength." Zero-sum in functioning: the one who "gives in" "loses self" to the other who "gains self."
- **Conflictive marriage** → the pattern when "both spouses fight for their rights." Conflict subsides when either gives in — i.e. conflict is the state in which the strength transfer is *not* settled.
- **Reluctant vs. volunteered adaptation (two distinct sub-mechanisms)** → "a large group of marriages in which differences are settled by one spouse who reluctantly 'gives in' after a brief period of contesting rights," and "another large group in which one spouse actively works to become the dependent one. This spouse volunteers to 'give in' and to become a 'no-self' to strengthen the self of the other, on whom he is dependent."
- **Incapacitation of the adaptive spouse** → a spouse who habitually gives in, reluctantly or voluntarily, "can reach a state of sufficient 'no-self' to become incapacitated with (1) a physical illness, (2) an emotional illness, or (3) a social dysfunction such as work inefficiency, drinking, and social irresponsibility." Three named symptom channels for the same deficit.
- **Symptom as absorber of the ego deficit** → a chronic illness in one spouse "seems to absorb the ego deficit between them," permanently stabilizing the marriage.
- **Outside relationships as stabilizers** → "Relationships to those outside the new family ego mass can influence the intensity of the process between the spouses." Named instances: retained emotional attachment to the parental ego masses tones the marital intensity down; a wife's work relationships keep the marriage relatively stabilized.
- **Loss or destabilization of the outside relationship** → "Termination of the work relationships, without a satisfactory replacement, can result in marital tension and conflict." And worse: "If the stabilizing outside relationship becomes unstabilized, it not only loses its stabilizing value, but it can become a source of added emotional pressure within the marriage." The same channel can flip sign.
- **Generational direction of the outside investment** → some spouses invest chiefly in the past generation (parents, aunts, uncles), some in the present generation (siblings, friends of their own age), some in the future generation (children, students); "Most will spread their important relationships to more than one generation."
- **Concentration vs. spread of the emotional problem** → "A small group of spouses will keep their emotional problems confined to one small area" — either exclusively in each other (giving a conflictive marriage or a chronically disabled spouse), or transmitted wholly to a child. "The average family maintains relationships in a number of areas, and the family receives support from, or transmits its emotional problems to, a number of areas." Spread is protective; concentration is pathogenic for whoever receives it.
- **Transmission of the parental problem to a child** → spouses "transmit the entire weight of the parental problem to one or more children," producing a calm marriage and "a major impairment in the child." In the schizophrenic case, transmitting "a major portion of the parental problem to the child" lets the parents "maintain a relatively calm adjustment until the child develops the psychosis and can no longer function."
- **Stabilizing-mechanism effectiveness dominates problem intensity** → "the clinical course can depend more on the effectiveness of mechanisms to stabilize the problem than on the intensity of the problem." Two named exceptions to the simple severity rule: very-low-identity spouses who find effective permanent stabilizers do well; high-identity spouses whose stabilizing effort fails "can experience almost as much anxiety and stress as a family with low identity."
  - **Correction `[p2]`: stabiliser availability is *not* independent of level; only the resulting course is.** The chapter states the coupling directly — "Spouses with relatively high levels of identity generally are able to find **a wider range of effective stabilizing mechanisms**." Level sets the *breadth of the stabiliser repertoire*; which stabiliser is in force, and whether it holds, sets the course. The two named exception classes are what happens at each end when repertoire breadth and realised effectiveness come apart.
  - **The stabilisers Bowen names for a low-identity marriage are both other people's symptoms `[p2]`.** His two worked examples of "fairly effective long-term or permanent stabilizing mechanisms" at very low identity are (a) transmitting "a major portion of the parental problem to the child," maintaining "a relatively calm adjustment until the child develops the psychosis," and (b) "A permanent physical incapacity in one spouse." Effectiveness is purchased with impairment in a member; it is not a free parameter.
  - **The symptom-stabiliser has two termination conditions, not one `[p2]`.** Pass 1 recorded only recovery. The chapter also bounds it by survival: the incapacity stabilises the marriage "as long as the incapacitated one lives." Both the cure and the death of the symptom-bearer end the stabilisation.
- **Dependent-appendage functioning (the pre-schizophrenic route)** → these people are "never able to 'tear themselves away' to achieve a workable level of 'pseudo-self'" and "continue always to function as dependent appendages of the family ego mass." Life adjustment "depends on the all-important attachment to another person who will guide and advise them, and from whom they can borrow enough self to function."
- **Transfer requires a waiting ego mass** → "any longer term separation from the parental ego mass is possible only when there is another waiting ego mass to which they can attach themselves." Departure is impossible without a destination.
- **Collapse on threat to the attachment** → they can hold dependent attachments in equilibrium for life, "but they are extremely vulnerable to any threatened or actual loss of the important other, and they can collapse into psychotic nothingness with life events that threaten or disrupt their dependent attachment." Note: *threatened* loss suffices; the trigger is an event about the relationship, not about the person.
- **The single scale** → "the entire range of human functioning on a single scale" from theoretical complete maturity at the top to "the severest forms of emotional illness at the bottom." All of the above are positions on, or consequences of position on, this one dimension.

## A5. Terminology

- **undifferentiated family ego mass** — the chapter's central term, and Bowen dates his search for it: "For eight years I have been looking for the best way to conceptualize the idea of a 'family emotional oneness.' The most useful concept found thus far is conveyed by the term undifferentiated family ego mass." Definition here: "a fused cluster of egos of individual family members, with a common ego boundary." He immediately concedes "There are some inaccuracies in the term" and keeps it on communicative grounds — it works on people who think in individual theory.
- **family emotional oneness** — the phenomenon the term above is trying to name; itself an extension of the earlier "symbiotic emotional oneness" between mother and patient.
- **fusion of selfs / ego fusion / emotionally fused** — the constituent process; obliteration of ego boundaries between members.
- **differentiation of self** — level of separation of self from the undifferentiated family ego mass, achieved (or not) in growing up. Bowen explicitly makes it interchangeable with two other words: "I would consider 'differentiation of self' to be equivalent to 'identity' or 'individuality.'" Two guardrails: do not confuse "identity" with the psychoanalytic "identification"; and avoid the notion of individuality as "different from."
- **identity** — used throughout the second half as the working synonym ("levels of identity," "a low-identity marriage," "families with the higher levels of identity"). *Note for later passes: "identity" is doing the whole job that "differentiation of self" does.* `[p2]` **Pass 1's frequency claim was wrong and is corrected.** Counted over the whole chapter the two are tied at **13 occurrences each** ("ego mass" dominates both at **34**). The real pattern is *sectional*, and sharper than a frequency count: from the stabiliser/prognosis discussion onward — every occurrence after the scale paragraph — the level word is **exclusively "identity"** (7 of 7), and "differentiation of self" never reappears. The developmental and definitional passages use "differentiation of self"; the prognostic passages use "identity."
- **basic level `[p2]`** — the chapter *does* name the basic level: "identical **basic** levels of differentiation of self," and "more **basic** attachment to his parents." What it never names is a *functional* level. Pass 1's claim that the chapter "never names" the distinction was half wrong; the correct statement is that one side of the pair is named and the other is only implied (equal selves at marriage → unequal share thereafter).
- **stabilizing mechanisms `[p2]`** — used as a named class of thing a family has, with a quantity attached to it ("effectiveness", "a wider range of"), not as a verb describing one arrangement. This is the term's first appearance in this sense in the corpus.
- **emotional morass of interdependence `[p2]`** — Bowen's phrase for the fused default state that the differentiating move has to break out of.
- **jigsaw puzzle of strength and weakness `[p2]`** — appears nowhere in Ch01–Ch06; it is this chapter's own image.
- **pseudo-self** — the substitute self established by "tearing away" from parents without differentiating; what merges in a low-level marriage.
- **pseudo-separation** — separation achieved by denial while still at home, or by physical distance, without differentiation.
- **no-self** — the end state of the habitually adaptive spouse; a state, reachable by degrees, at which incapacitation occurs.
- **contained emotional unit** — Bowen's description of the mature person: able to maintain ego boundaries under stress without fusing.
- **emotional Siamese twins** — the extreme closeness pole of the mother-patient cycle.
- **Achilles' heel** — the close emotional relationship, as the specific vulnerability of low-differentiation people.
- **the family as the patient** — "The alternative was to treat the family, not the individual, as the patient."
- **the family ego mass considered equivalent to a single ego** — the therapeutic construal, distinct from the descriptive definition.
- **the stronger side / the weak side of the family ego** — the internal asymmetry of the mass; the side that creates and maintains the sickness vs. the side that carries it.
- **the family member already designated "sick" or "patient" by the family process** — symptom-bearer named as an assignment made by the family, not a property of the person.
- **observational blindness** — Bowen's name for the investigator's inability to see data that does not fit his frame.
- **jigsaw puzzle of strength and weakness** — his image for distributed, tradeable functioning.
- **Terms conspicuously ABSENT here** (record for the comparative pass): no *triangle*, no *nuclear family emotional system*, no *family projection process*, no *multigenerational transmission process*, no *emotional cutoff*, no *sibling position*, no *scale of differentiation* as a numbered scale, no *chronic anxiety*, no *societal emotional process*. Several of the *phenomena* those later terms name are described here in plain language (transmission to a child, distance masking attachment, conflict shifting between dyads) but none is given a term.
  - `[p2]` **Absences verified by exhaustive string search over the chapter file**, not by impression: `triangl` 0 · `triad` 0 · `three` 0 · `project` 0 (in any form, including "projection") · `multigenerational` 0 · `cutoff` / `cut off` 0. `third` occurs once and is purely ordinal ("third by the intensity of the ego fusion"). `sibling` occurs 6 times, always as a kinship noun and never as a *position* with properties. `anxiet` occurs **twice** in the entire chapter — "the anxiety of fusion" and "almost as much anxiety and stress as a family with low identity" — so anxiety here is a *consequence* (of closeness, and of failed stabilisation), never a driver, never a stock, and never chronic.

## A6. Structure and roles

- **The parental pair is structurally privileged and symmetric.** "The two spouses, or the two parents in families with children, are equally and maximally involved in the family ego mass"; for clinical purposes the mass "always involves the father and mother, maximally and equally." No asymmetry between mother and father is claimed here — and this is an explicit correction of Bowen's own earlier mother-centred view: fathers were "as intimately involved as the mothers."
- **One child is drawn in; the others are not.** "In many families the one child who is most dependent on the parents is as involved in the parental ego mass as the parents. The other children are usually more differentiated." Selection of *which* child is not explained in this chapter.
- **The symptom-bearer is a family-assigned position.** The member "already designated 'sick' or 'patient' by the family process." "The stronger side of the family ego plays an important part in creating and maintaining the 'sickness' in the weak side."
- **Sibling divergence within one family.** Future parents of schizophrenics, as children, "do not begin the steady process of 'growing away' from their parents as do their more differentiated siblings." Siblings of the same parents land at different levels. `[p2]` **And no rule is given anywhere in the chapter for what sets a child's level.** Not birth order, not sex, not parental level, not which parent. The only structural statement is that the most dependent child is as involved as the parents and "the other children are usually more differentiated" — a consequence of involvement, stated without a cause. Any generational update rule in the model must be sourced elsewhere; this chapter is silent, not confirmatory.
- **Membership is by emotional involvement, not kinship or address.** Three nested definitions are given: *theoretically* the mass extends back through the extended family to "all members of the extended families who still have unresolved emotional dependencies on each other"; *for practical purposes* it is "the family members in active daily living contact with each other" (for some families the household; for others living separately but in daily contact, it extends beyond the household); *for clinical purposes* it is "those family members most involved in the family emotional interdependence." And nonrelatives qualify: "Live-in servants can be more emotionally fused into the family emotional system than certain blood relatives"; hospital staff serve as fusion targets in the distance phase.
  - `[p2]` **These three definitions are scope conventions for the reader, not the stress mechanism.** They sit in the *Family Psychotherapy* section and answer "whom do I count as in the mass for this purpose"; the stress-dependent radius sits in the theory section and answers "who is in the mass right now." Pass 1 kept them in separate bullets but did not mark the distinction, and it matters: the radius claim is a *dynamic* claim and the three definitions are a *static* one. The practical definition — "family members in active daily living contact with each other," extending beyond the household where daily contact does — is also the one place in the chapter where contact frequency enters membership at all; note it governs *whom to count*, never the strength of the tie (cf. the runaway who may have more attachment, and the quiescent-but-active sibling far away).
- **Generational direction of a spouse's key outside relationships** — past generation (parents, aunts, uncles), present generation (siblings, friends of the same age group), or future generation (children, students); most spread over more than one.
- **Who moves first.** Two candidates in this chapter. In the family: "When either spouse is able to define and maintain a more definite identity from this amorphous feeling mass, the first step in the recovery process is under way" — either spouse, not a designated one. In the therapy: the therapist directs effort at "that portion of the family ego mass most capable of change," i.e. both parents as a team, or "the one most motivated parent until the other expresses a wish to join the effort."
- **The dependent person needs a holder.** Life adjustment "depends on the all-important attachment to another person who will guide and advise them, and from whom they can borrow enough self to function." That role can be a spouse, and transfer between holders needs "another waiting ego mass."

# STREAM B — How to do therapy  (→ the coaching agent)

## B1. What the therapist does

- **Treat the family, not the individual, as the patient.** Stated as the alternative chosen at the 1954–55 turning point.
- **Relate to the family ego mass as if it were a single ego** — "The family psychotherapist relates himself to the family ego in the same way a psychotherapist relates himself to the individual ego in individual psychotherapy," and the therapy is "directed to the family ego mass without specific regard for the individual family members involved... at the time."
- **Choose the attendance for each hour deliberately, on three criteria**: "the dynamics within the family ego mass, the immediate therapeutic goal, and the stage of family psychotherapy." Permissible configurations: all involved members present, any combination, or a single family member.
- **When working through a single member, address the most intact part.** The analogue is "the individual therapist who relates himself to the most intact portion of a person's ego, or to the mature side of the individual ego."
- **Direct effort at the portion of the family ego mass most capable of change**, because "the goal is to bring about the most rapid possible change."
- **Prefer both parents working as a team**; if not possible, work with "the one most motivated parent until the other expresses a wish to join the effort."
- **Help the stronger side of the family assume responsibility for the weaker side.** Stated as the goal in place of treating the weak side.
- **Shift sides as the work proceeds** — "working with family members alone, or with various combinations of family members, shifting from one side of the family ego to another, as the differentiating process proceeds"; and with severe child problems, "I shift about, utilizing any available family strength, until a more stable family structure is possible."
- **Push each member toward two specific behaviours**, which are the operational content of differentiation: "maintaining a self in the face of emotional pressure from the other, and maintaining responsibility for self without so much emotional demand on the other."
  - `[p2]` **Each half is the exact negation of a named default.** The chapter states the default immediately before: being "the way the other wants them to be to improve the functioning of the other" (negated by *maintain a self under the other's pressure*) and "demanding that the other be different to enhance the functioning of self" (negated by *maintain responsibility for self without so much emotional demand on the other*). So the differentiating move is not an addition to the repertoire — it is the suppression of two specific ongoing behaviours, and it should be modelled as such.
- **State the over-all goal in mass terms `[p2]`** — "to help the involved family members to differentiate clearly defined 'selfs' from the undifferentiated ego mass." The objective is stated on the structural variable, never on symptom or on comfort.
- **Conflictive marriage as presenting problem:** "work with one alone or each separately until they begin calmly working together."
- **Symptomatic spouse who seeks help for self:** "work with this one alone until the 'sick' one is able to maintain a 'self,' with both working together on the problem."
- **Child as the identified patient:** aim for "a stage when the two parents can work together on their part of the family problem without involving the child."
- **Severe child problem (borderline psychosis, psychosis, severe character defect):** "work with each parent alone to help each maintain a more definite position in relating to the child."
- **Carry successful cases into a final joint stage:** the two spouses "working together in analysis of deeper intrapsychic problems in each."
- **Commit totally to the family orientation** — Bowen's own move was to discontinue individual psychotherapy "in spite of protests" and commit to "a good hard try at family psychotherapy before returning to individual."

## B2. What the therapist must NOT do

- **Do not compromise between individual and family orientation.** "The 'individual-family' issue is an 'either-or' proposition. Any compromise in maintaining a strict family orientation is immediately utilized by family dynamics to make it an individual approach, and the unique advantages inherent in family psychotherapy can be lost."
- **Do not run individual psychotherapy alongside** — the individual therapy of family members was discontinued, not tapered, and the concurrent arrangement is precisely what "compromise" refers to.
- **Usually avoid a relationship with the designated patient.** "I usually avoid a relationship with the family member already designated 'sick' or 'patient' by the family process."
- **Do not become directly responsible for the "sick" family member** — to do so "is to miss the part played by the rest of the family and to permit a continuation of the process."
- **Do not rely on the all-family communication meeting as the method.** Parents and all the children meeting "to learn to communicate and to verbalize feelings" is "extremely beneficial in relieving symptoms and in creating a temporary better feeling and attitude," but Bowen "[has] not found it helpful in a continuing family psychotherapy effort designed to resolve deep problems."
- **Do not keep the child in the room once the parents can work without him** — the child improves "without participation in the family psychotherapy."
- **Do not assume the "sick" side is where the work is** — the sickness is created and maintained by the stronger side.

## B3. Sequencing and preconditions

- **Precondition for the fastest route:** the two spouses must be "able to work as a team." Only then are many peripheral issues avoidable.
- **Conflictive marriage:** one alone or each separately → *until* they begin calmly working together → joint work. The transition condition is stated ("calmly"), not a duration.
- **Symptomatic spouse:** that spouse alone → *until* he/she "is able to maintain a 'self'" → both working together.
- **Child patient (ordinary severity):** parents work together on their own part → child improves automatically. The child's improvement is downstream, not a step.
- **Child patient (severe: borderline psychosis, psychosis, severe character defect):** the parents *cannot* proceed without the child at first, "so much of the parental self is invested in the child" → therapist shifts about using whatever family strength appears → "until a more stable family structure is possible" → often each parent alone, to help each "maintain a more definite position in relating to the child" → several such families "proceeded on to an orderly analysis of the parental problem." A four-stage path with an explicit blocking precondition.
- **First step of recovery, generally:** "When either spouse is able to define and maintain a more definite identity from this amorphous feeling mass, the first step in the recovery process is under way."
- **Final stage, invariably:** "The final stages of successful family psycho[therapy] invariably includes the two spouses working together on their individual and joint problems" / "invariably involve the two spouses working together in analysis of deeper intrapsychic problems in each." Deep intrapsychic work is the *last* stage, not the first.
- **Stage as an input to attendance:** who is invited depends in part on "the stage of family psychotherapy" — the method is explicitly phased.
- **Durations:** none given for therapy. The only timescales in the chapter are research timescales (see A4).

## B4. Stated failure modes

- **Individual psychotherapy of an involved member** → tension tones down during treatment, recurs when treatment ends.
- **Any compromise of the family orientation** → the family converts it into an individual approach; the advantages of family psychotherapy are lost.
- **Therapist takes direct responsibility for the sick member** → the therapist misses the part played by the rest of the family and permits the process to continue.
- **All-family communication/feeling-verbalization meetings** → temporary symptom relief and better feeling only; no resolution of deep problems.
- **Low-identity families in treatment** → they seek "immediate relief and another adaptive mechanism that works a little better than the last"; slight relief produces glowing praise; failure produces acceptance and abandonment ("leave the future course to chance").
- **Waiting until the family asks** → families come only after "its own stabilizing mechanisms have failed and family efforts to solve the problem result in 'making it worse'." Presentation is a late-stage event by construction.
- **Recovery of the disabled spouse as a destabilizer** → the harmonious marriage holds "as long as the disabled spouse does not recover." Symptom removal is a threat to the marital equilibrium.
- **The parents' calm ends when the child decompensates** → they stay calm "until the child develops the psychosis and can no longer function." The stabilizer is consumed.
- **Observational blindness in the clinician/investigator** → the frame determines what can be seen; Bowen states this cost him years of visible evidence.

## B5. What the technique assumes about the family

Each rule of technique, converted back into the claim about family functioning that would have to be true for it to work.

1. **"Relate to the family ego mass through any one sufficiently involved member."** → The family's emotional state is a *shared, single-valued quantity*, and change injected at any sufficiently coupled node propagates to the whole. A model that gives each person an independent state and only pairwise links cannot reproduce this; it requires a family-level variable that individuals are coupled into.
2. **"Choose attendance by dynamics, goal, and stage."** → Which members are in contact at a given moment *is itself a control variable* that changes the outcome. Contact structure is not fixed by kinship; it is manipulable, and different structures make different moves available.
3. **"Avoid the designated patient; the stronger side creates and maintains the sickness in the weak side."** → Symptom location is an *assignment made by the system*, not a property of the person. It follows that symptom-bearing is reassignable, and that removing the symptom without changing the assignment mechanism simply relocates it.
4. **"Help the stronger side assume responsibility for the weaker side."** → Functioning is a *transferable, near-conserved quantity* within the mass. One member's strength is partly borrowed from another's deficit; therefore therapy that adds strength to the weak member without addressing the strong member's over-share does nothing to the total.
5. **"When the parents work on their own part, the child improves automatically."** → The child's symptom has *no independent generator*. It is driven entirely by the state of the parental dyad and the amount of parental problem routed to him. The child is a sink, not a source.
6. **"Any compromise is immediately utilized by family dynamics to make it an individual approach."** → The family *actively detects and reclassifies the intervention*, and has a restoring force pulling any arrangement back toward the individual/symptom-bearer framing. This is a homeostatic response with a fast time constant ("immediately"), not passive inertia.
7. **"Individual psychotherapy tones tension down and it recurs on termination."** → The therapist occupies *the same structural slot as any other stabilizing outside relationship* — the wife's job, the attachment back to parents. He tones the process down while attached, and his loss without replacement raises tension. A coach in the model is therefore not outside the system; he is an outside-relationship node with the same stabilize-then-destabilize signature.
8. **"Direct effort at the portion most capable of change."** → Change capacity is *unequally distributed* inside the mass, and the family's rate of change is governed by its most capable member rather than by its average or its weakest.
9. **"Work with one spouse alone until he can maintain a self, then together."** → Differentiation must be achieved *against the other's emotional pressure*, and the pressure is strong enough that separated contact is sometimes required for the move to be possible at all. Emotional pressure from the partner is a real force term that suppresses self-definition.
10. **"The final stages invariably involve the two spouses together."** → Resolution is a property of *the top dyad*, not of the symptom-bearer or of any single person. No path to resolution runs through the child.
11. **"Attendance depends on the stage."** → Therapy is a *phased process with stage-gated available moves*: a move that works in a late stage is unavailable or counterproductive early.
12. **"With a severe child problem the parents cannot proceed without the child, because so much parental self is invested in him."** → *Invested self is a quantity* that binds a parent to a child and constrains which moves the parent can make. Above some level of investment the parent-only configuration is not viable.
13. **"Relate to the most intact portion of the ego / the mature side."** → Each person contains *simultaneously differentiated and fused portions*; the person is not a single scalar level in the moment, and the therapist's choice of which portion to address changes the response.
14. **"The first step is either spouse defining and maintaining a more definite identity, under pressure and without demand on the other."** → The elementary unit of change is a *two-part behavioural move by one person* — hold position under pressure, AND withdraw emotional demand — and it is available to either member of the dyad, not to a designated one.
15. **"Communication and feeling-verbalization meetings relieve symptoms but do not resolve."** → Symptom level and structural level are *separable*: an intervention can lower the symptom without touching the fusion that generates it. Symptom relief is therefore not a valid readout of structural change — and this is the chapter's clearest warning for any outcome metric in a simulation.
16. **"Families come when their stabilizers have failed and their own efforts are making it worse."** → The family's self-correction is *sign-reversing under load*: what worked as a stabilizer becomes an amplifier past some point, and that crossing is what produces help-seeking.
17. **"Higher-identity families are better prospects and persist; lower-identity families quit after slight relief."** → Level of differentiation determines not only pathology but the *engagement/persistence parameter* with an intervention. The same coaching move applied at different levels yields different retention, independent of its effect.
18. `[p2]` **"Each too dependent on the other to risk becoming a clear self."** → The differentiating move carries a *cost that scales with the actor's dependence on the other*, so the propensity to attempt it falls as fusion rises. Combined with A6's "either spouse" this is a specific shape: the move is available to anyone in the dyad, and its expected cost is highest exactly where its benefit is greatest. A model in which propensity rises monotonically with functioning gets this wrong in both directions.
19. `[p2]` **"Spouses become so involved in being the way the other wants them to be… and demanding that the other be different… that neither is responsible for self."** → Fused agents run a *continuously active pair of cross-directed behaviours*, not an idle state punctuated by moves. Responsibility-for-self is the quantity that gets consumed by them, and it is what the coach's two-part move restores. The model currently has no default behaviour for a person who is not making a move; this says the default is itself load-bearing and is what generates the fused equilibrium.
20. `[p2]` **"Spouses with relatively high levels of identity generally are able to find a wider range of effective stabilizing mechanisms."** → The *repertoire of available stabilisers is gated by level*, while which one is in force and whether it holds is a separate state. Level therefore enters the model twice on different time constants: slowly, through what the family can reach for; fast, through whether the current arrangement is holding.

# CROSS-CUTTING

## X1. Contradictions and revisions

- **Bowen revises his own therapeutic premise, in the first person.** "At that time I still believed that the only way to emotional maturity was the careful analysis of the transference relationship between the patient and the therapist. I believe now that family psychotherapy can be more than a preparation for eventual individual psychotherapy for each family member."
- **The mother-symbiosis hypothesis is superseded.** His earlier hypothesis built the whole of schizophrenia out of the mother-patient symbiosis; here that relationship is demoted to "a fragment of a larger family emotional system in which fathers were as intimately involved as the mothers." He explicitly diagnoses *why* he made that error — the symbiosis "had already been described in the literature as an extension of individual theory," so it was the only thing his frame let him see.
- **The scope is explicitly widened:** from "a family concept of schizophrenia" to "a family theory of emotional illness," and the psychotherapy adapted "to the entire range of emotional illness." This names the shift away from the earlier chapters' subject matter.
- **He concedes the central term is inaccurate.** "There are some inaccuracies in the term but it aptly describes the over-all family dynamics" — retained because it communicates to people who think in individual theory, i.e. on rhetorical rather than descriptive grounds.
- **Internal tension: single ego vs. structured interior.** The therapy is "directed to the family ego mass without specific regard for the individual family members involved," and the mass "is considered equivalent to a single ego." Yet the same section requires the therapist to distinguish stronger side from weak side, designated patient from non-patient, the most motivated parent from the other, and the portion "most capable of change." The mass is treated as one ego for the purpose of the relationship, and as a differentiated structure for the purpose of targeting.
- **The severity rule is qualified as soon as it is stated.** "As a general rule, spouses with lower levels of differentiation of self will have more intense ego fusions and more serious problems... However, the clinical course can depend more on the effectiveness of mechanisms to stabilize the problem than on the intensity of the problem." Differentiation level is thus *not* a sufficient predictor of course.
- **Does fusion occur at the top of the scale or not? `[p2]`** Three statements in the same section do not reconcile. (a) Of well-differentiated spouses: "These spouses do **not** become involved in the 'fusion of selfs' that occurs in marriages of less differentiated spouses" — absence. (b) Of the marital fusion process: "This same process, **on a less intense level**, occurs in people with higher levels of differentiation of self" — presence, attenuated. (c) "Theoretically, the fusion is present to some degree in **all** families except those in which family members have attained complete emotional maturity" — presence everywhere below an unreachable ceiling. (a) and (b) describe the same population oppositely unless "well-differentiated" is silently read as "completely mature," which (c) says is theoretical only. Pass 1 recorded claim 8 as a clean monotonic and missed this. For the model it is the difference between fusion decaying to zero at high C and fusion asymptoting to a small positive floor; this chapter supports both and settles neither.
- **Differentiation vs. distance.** The claim that a runaway may be more attached than a stay-at-home sibling contradicts any reading of differentiation as physical or contact-based separation; pseudo-separation "reinforced by physical distance" is named as a form of *non*-differentiation.
- **Source-text corruption to note (OCR):** "each 'refuse' with other family members" almost certainly reads *re-fuse*; "the final stages of successful family psychology" almost certainly reads *psychotherapy*; "Thre is another large group" reads *There*; "schiziphrenic" reads *schizophrenic*. Recorded so a later pass does not treat "refuse" as a term of art.

## X2. Hedges and uncertainty

- "The most useful concept found thus far" and "There are some inaccuracies in the term" — the central construct is presented as provisional after eight years of searching.
- "Theoretically, the fusion is present to some degree in all families except those in which family members have attained complete emotional maturity" and "Theoretically, a mature person is a contained emotional unit" — the top of the scale is flagged as theoretical, i.e. never observed. "Theoretical complete maturity" is likewise parenthesised as theoretical.
- Competing explanations for his own earlier blindness, resolved by belief not evidence: "One can offer support to the thesis that this relationship was so noisy and obvious that it obscured the view of the larger family. **I believe** attention went to the mother-patient relationship because the larger view was obscured by fixed individual thinking."
- "perhaps even during the emotional interchanges during courtship" — the timing of marital fusion is a guess.
- "the best one can hope for is to reach a stage when the two parents can work together" — a stated ceiling on outcome in child cases.
- "In these instances, **I shift about**, utilizing any available family strength" — the severe-child procedure is admittedly improvised rather than specified.
- "Several of these families have proceeded on to an orderly analysis of the parental problem" — "several," no denominator.
- "Family psychotherapy provides the greatest flexibility and the best results of any psychotherapeutic method **in my experience**" — the strongest outcome claim in the chapter, explicitly resting on personal clinical experience alone.
- "I **expect** one of the greatest dividends from 'the family' **may be** the new insights it provides" — doubly hedged.
- `[p2]` Hedges pass 1 did not record, all of them on load-bearing mechanisms: "thereafter one spouse **usually** functions with more than an equal share" (the transfer is typical, not universal); "A marriage **might** become permanently stabilized"; the chronic illness "**seems to** absorb the ego deficit"; "the clinical course **can** depend more on the effectiveness of mechanisms"; "Spouses with relatively high levels of identity **generally** are able to find a wider range." Every quantitative-sounding statement in the theory section carries a modal.
- The state of the field: "currently in an unstructured transitional state of chaos," with "little agreement about what makes up the 'family' part of family psychotherapy and even less agreement about... the 'psychotherapy' part."
- Communication difficulty admitted as a limit on the theory's transmissibility: "First, it was difficult to get it clear in my own thinking. Then it was even more difficult to present it so it could be heard."

## X3. Unprompted observations

- **Observation is theory-limited.** The Darwin/prehistoric-bones passage is a methodological claim with direct force for modelling: the phenomena a frame cannot represent will not be recorded as data, they will be recorded as artifacts. Anything the simulation cannot represent, its user will not notice missing.
- **The ego mass has a stress-dependent radius.** Calm → few most-involved members; stress → extended family and nonrelatives drawn in. Membership is a dynamic function of load, not a fixed roster. This is the single most simulation-ready idea in the chapter that has no named term attached to it.
- **Kinship does not determine membership.** Live-in servants can be more fused than blood relatives; hospital staff become fusion targets. Any model keyed on the family tree alone will get the wrong set.
- **Conflict appears conserved across dyads.** Conflict between A and B disappearing "with the simultaneous development of conflict between two other family members" describes transfer, not resolution — and it is a *dyad-to-dyad* transfer among four people, the nearest thing in this chapter to a shifting-triangle observation, stated without the word.
- **Cross-person somatization.** One member becoming physically ill in response to emotional stress in another puts physical symptom on the same channel as emotional symptom and social dysfunction — consistent with the three incapacitation routes of the adaptive spouse.
- **Departure requires a destination.** "Longer term separation from the parental ego mass is possible only when there is another waiting ego mass to which they can attach themselves." Attachment transfer, not attachment release. Implies moves that fail silently when no target exists.
- **Threatened loss is as potent as actual loss** for the dependent person — the trigger is a change in the relationship's status, not an event in the world.
- **Mind-reading in the closeness phase** is reported as a plain observation ("could accurately know the thoughts, feelings, fantasies, and dreams"), i.e. fusion is claimed to have an information-theoretic signature, not only an affective one.
- **Recovery can be destabilizing.** A marriage stable on a disabled spouse holds only while the disability holds. Improvement in one member is, in this framework, a perturbation to be absorbed somewhere.
- **The therapist is inside the system.** The individual-therapy-relapse observation and the outside-relationship mechanism are structurally the same claim; Bowen does not say so, but the chapter contains both halves.
- **Work appears only as a relationship, never as an economy. `[p2]`** The word "work" occurs 28 times in the chapter and not once denotes income, money, property or material resources. Its three senses are: emotional attachments at work as a stabilising outside relationship ("A wife who works might keep the marital relationship relatively stabilized **as long as she maintains the emotional attachments at work**" — the value is explicitly the attachment, and its loss is a loss of *relationship*, not of earnings); "work inefficiency" as one instance of the social-dysfunction symptom channel; and therapeutic work. The chapter has no financial, economic or material per-person quantity of any kind, and its one marker of household wealth — live-in servants — is introduced solely as evidence about *membership*.
- **Anxiety is an output here, not a state variable. `[p2]`** It occurs twice: as the cost that drives distance ("the anxiety of fusion") and as an outcome measure of a failed stabiliser. Nothing accumulates it, transports it, or discharges it. The chapter's causal spine is level of differentiation → intensity of fusion → stabilising mechanism and its effectiveness → clinical course, with anxiety read off the end. Any model whose dynamics are driven *by* an anxiety stock is following a later Bowen (Ch09, Ch17), not this one.
- **Marriage is not the trigger; living-together interdependence is.** "At the time of the marriage, **or at the time they establish a living-together interdependence**" — the fusion event is defined by contact structure, not by legal status.

## M. Model implications (first cut)

- Undifferentiated family ego mass with a common ego boundary → `Family` → needs a family-level state variable that individuals are coupled into, not merely a graph of pairwise relationships.
- Ego-mass membership expands under stress, contracts in calm → `Family` / `event-kind` → membership should be a computed, stress-dependent set (a coupling radius), recomputed each tick; currently likely a fixed roster → **`unmodelled`**.
- Nonrelatives (servants, staff, friends) can be members; blood relatives can be outside → `Family` / `Person` → membership predicate must be emotional involvement, not kinship edges → **`unmodelled`**.
- Level-matched mate selection ("identical basic levels of differentiation") → `Person` / `event-kind:marriage` → pairing rule for family formation; also the mechanism by which level persists across generations.
- Differentiation level on a single continuous scale, with ordinal anchors (neurosis mid, character disorder lower half, parent-of-schizophrenic far low, schizophrenia extreme low) → `Person` → a single scalar per person; the anchors give ordinal validation targets for a `test`.
- Basic level fixed vs. functioning level variable (equal selves at marriage → unequal functioning share thereafter) → `Person` → two distinct attributes are required: basic level, and current functional level. The chapter forces this distinction even though it never names it.
- Zero-sum transfer of functioning within the dyad (one gains self, the other loses self) → `Relationship` → an explicit strength-transfer term with near-conservation; test that total functioning is approximately conserved while individual levels diverge.
- Conflictive marriage = both fight for rights; conflict subsides when one gives in → `Relationship` / `move:GIVE_IN` → conflict is the unsettled-transfer state; giving in resolves conflict and moves self.
- Reluctant vs. volunteered adaptation as two distinct routes to the same no-self state → `Relationship` → an adaptive-posture parameter, not just a transfer magnitude → possibly **`unmodelled`**.
- Three incapacitation channels — physical illness, emotional illness, social dysfunction (work inefficiency, drinking, social irresponsibility) → `Person` / `event-kind:symptom` → symptom expression needs a channel selector, with the same underlying deficit driving all three.
- Symptom absorbs the ego deficit and stabilizes the marriage; recovery destabilizes it → `Family` / `test` → symptom must feed back *negatively* onto family tension; a test should show that curing the symptom without changing the deficit raises tension.
- Ordered determinants of family course: (1) spouse dynamics, (2) outside relationships, (3) fusion intensity → `Family` → gives an explicit weighting order for whatever terms drive the family trajectory; directly testable as a sensitivity ordering.
- Outside relationships tone down marital intensity; their loss without replacement raises tension; their destabilization *adds* pressure → `Relationship` / `event-kind:outside_relationship_change` → outside ties need a sign that can flip, not a fixed protective bonus → likely **`unmodelled`**.
- Generational direction of outside investment (past / present / future generation) → `Person` → an allocation vector over three targets; "most spread over more than one."
- Concentration vs. spread of the emotional problem determines *where* impairment lands (spouse conflict, disabled spouse, or impaired child) → `Family` → a routing/allocation mechanism, with spread protective and concentration pathogenic. This is the chapter's core projection mechanism, unnamed.
- Transmitting the parental problem to a child ⇒ calm marriage AND impaired child → `Family` / `test` → an inverse-coupling invariant: marital calm and child impairment should move in opposite directions when the transmission channel is active.
- One child most involved, other siblings more differentiated → `Person` / `Family` → sibling divergence within a family; the *selection rule* for which child is not given here → **`unmodelled`** (flag for later chapters).
- Effectiveness of stabilizing mechanisms outweighs intensity of the problem for clinical course → `Family` → stabilizer efficacy is a separate parameter from differentiation level; low-level families with good stabilizers should look calm.
- High-level family whose stabilizer fails ≈ as anxious as a low-level family → `test` → peak anxiety is not a monotonic function of level; level sets the baseline and the recovery, not the ceiling.
- Symbiotic cycling: closeness → fusion anxiety → distance → re-approach → `Relationship` → an oscillator with approach drive and loss-of-self cost; distance is the regulator, not an outcome.
- Conflict disappearing in one dyad as it appears in another → `Triangle` / `Family` → dyad-to-dyad transfer of conflict; the chapter's proto-triangle observation, stated without the term.
- Cross-person somatization (illness in A from stress in B) → `Person` / `event-kind` → symptom generation must accept another person's stress as input.
- Dependent-appendage person: borrows self from an important other; collapses on threatened or actual loss → `Person` / `event-kind:attachment_threat` → collapse trigger keyed on *threat*, not only on loss.
- Separation from the parental ego mass requires "another waiting ego mass" → `move:LEAVE_HOME` → the move must fail or not fire when no destination attachment exists → **`unmodelled`**.
- Physical distance / cutoff is pseudo-separation, and may mask *greater* attachment → `Person` / `test` → distance must not be scored as differentiation; an adversarial test where a maximally distant person scores *lower* on differentiation.
- Mind-reading in the closeness phase → `Relationship` → an information-coupling term distinct from affective coupling → **`unmodelled`**.
- Therapist relates to the family ego mass through any one member → `coach` → coaching input applied to one person must propagate to the family variable.
- Coach avoids the designated patient; works with the stronger side / most motivated member → `coach` → target selection rule: choose by change capacity and motivation, explicitly excluding the symptom-bearer.
- Rate of change set by the most capable member, not the average → `coach` / `Family` → change rate should read the max, not the mean.
- Any compromise of the family stance is immediately converted back to an individual approach → `Family` / `coach` → a fast restoring force that penalises mixed coaching stances; a coach that hedges should measurably lose effect → **`unmodelled`**.
- Coaching relieves tension while active and it recurs on termination → `coach` / `test` → the coach occupies the same slot as any stabilizing outside relationship; a test should show relapse on coach withdrawal absent a structural change.
- Elementary differentiation move: hold a self under the other's emotional pressure AND reduce emotional demand on the other → `move:DEFINE_SELF` → two components, both required; available to either spouse.
- Sequence: individual work until one can maintain a self → both work together → final stage of joint deeper work → `coach` → stage machine with explicit gating conditions rather than a session count.
- Parents work on their part without the child ⇒ child improves without attending → `Family` / `test` → a child-symptom test in which no intervention touches the child.
- Severe child problem: too much parental self invested in the child for parents to proceed alone → `Triangle` / `coach` → an investment quantity that gates which coaching configurations are viable → **`unmodelled`**.
- Symptom relief without structural change (communication meetings) → `test` → symptom level and fusion level must be separately observable, and symptom relief must be rejectable as an outcome measure.
- Help-seeking occurs only when stabilizers have failed and self-correction is making it worse → `Family` / `event-kind:help_seeking` → an entry trigger where the family's own corrective effort has reversed sign.
- Level of identity determines persistence with treatment (high stays to resolution; low quits after slight relief) → `coach` / `Person` → an engagement/retention parameter tied to level, separate from treatment effect.
- No numbers anywhere in the chapter — all quantities are ordinal or verbal → `test` → chapter 7 can supply ordering constraints and sign constraints for calibration, but no magnitudes; do not source parameter values from it.
